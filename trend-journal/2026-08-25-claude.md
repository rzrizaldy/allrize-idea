# RenewalRecord: Enrollment-Consent Evidence Capture for US Subscription Businesses Under State Auto-Renewal Laws — 2026-08-25

**Contributor:** Claude (Cowork scheduled task)
**File:** `trend-journal/2026-08-25-claude.md`
**Market:** United States first. The demand driver is a US state and municipal statutory patchwork plus US plaintiff-bar economics. Indonesia relevance: not established, and the mechanism does not transfer.
**Motion:** B2B. A subscription merchant pays out of a legal or engineering budget. The developer or growth engineer installs it. Outside counsel and the finance owner are the people who feel the pain.
**Verdict:** **Watch**

---

## Summary

Every US state auto-renewal law turns on the same factual question: what exactly did this specific consumer see on the enrollment screen, at the moment they consented, on the date they signed up. Liability is judged against the checkout page as it was rendered then, not as it renders today. Almost no subscription business can answer that question for a customer who enrolled two years ago, which is precisely the class period a plaintiff firm will plead.

RenewalRecord would be a snippet plus API that issues a durable, timestamped certificate for each subscription enrollment: the rendered disclosure block, its measured position relative to the consent control, the consent event, the consumer's state, the post-transaction acknowledgment that was sent, and the notice schedule that followed. When a demand letter arrives, the merchant produces a per-customer evidence packet instead of reconstructing history from a git log.

The trend is verified and durable. The legal mechanism is verified and specific. The gap in the incumbent's coverage is verified. What is not verified is that merchants will pay for this before they are sued, and that is the reason this is a Watch and not a Build.

- Louisiana's "Click to Cancel Act" takes effect **2027-01-01**, with penalties up to $500 per violation, a mandatory 30-day cure period after written AG notice, and a requirement to maintain "reasonable business records" demonstrating consumer consent for at least one year from contract formation. The Act broadly exempts businesses with fewer than 50 employees or under $5 million annual gross revenue, though Kelley Drye notes other provisions in the statute conflict on that point.

Source: [Kelley Drye, Summer 2026 Autorenewal Roundup](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/summer-2026-autorenewal-roundup-nyc-and-louisiana-enact-new-regulatory-requirements)

**Verified evidence.** Churnkey's own state guide (July 1, 2026) adds Minnesota (effective 2025, uniquely restricting save offers) and Virginia (adds conspicuous online cancel and cancel-as-easy-as-signup from July 1, 2026). ## 2. Customer and JTBD

**Who exactly.** A US subscription merchant with between roughly $1 million and $50 million in annual recurring revenue that enrolls consumers online: DTC consumables, meal kits, streaming and media, fitness and gym memberships, prosumer SaaS, membership sites. Kelley Drye and the Jeffer Mangels article both identify meal kits, streaming services, and gym memberships as the categories actually receiving demand letters.

**Who pays.** The company, from a legal or engineering budget. **Who uses.** A developer installs the snippet once. **Who feels the pain.** The founder or GC who receives the demand letter, and the outside counsel who has to answer it.

**The job.** "When a plaintiff's lawyer asserts that our checkout did not display the five required disclosures in visual proximity to the consent button on the day their client signed up, I need to be able to show what our page actually looked like on that day, for that customer, without hoping someone kept a screenshot."

**The secondary job.** "Tell me which of my subscribers are in states whose notice rules I am currently violating, and send the notices on the right schedule."

**Inference, from the JMM guidance below.** This job today is done reactively and badly. Counsel's own advice on receiving a letter is to go capture screenshots of the *current* site. That preserves today's page. It does not answer what the class period looked like, which is the question that actually drives settlement value.

## 3. Pain evidence

**Verified evidence.** Jeffer Mangels & Mitchell LLP, April 22, 2026, describing demand-letter practice against subscription businesses:

> "Plaintiffs have been sending demand letters and filing lawsuits alleging violations of California's Automatic Renewal Law (ARL) ... These demands focus on online checkout flows for recurring subscriptions where the company allegedly fails to provide the required 'clear and conspicuous' disclosures about automatic renewal terms right next to the 'PAY NOW' button."

> "These demands are part of a steady influx of ARL-based claims targeting meal kits, streaming services, gym memberships, and other recurring subscription model businesses. While not every letter results in an immediate lawsuit, many do."

> "Preserve evidence immediately. Capture screenshots and records of your current website checkout flow, subscription enrollment pages, confirmation emails, account settings, and cancellation options."

> "Because the requirements are technical and strictly construed, even well-intentioned businesses using standard e-commerce platforms can find themselves exposed."

The article reproduces an excerpt of an actual demand letter from Kevin J. Cole of KJC Law Group, alleging the checkout page did not display the five mandatory automatic-renewal disclosures in visual proximity to the consent prompt. Source: [JMM, ARL/CLRA Demand Letters Against Subscription-Based Businesses](https://articles.jeffer.com/2026/04/22/arl-clra-demand-letters-against-subscription-based-businesses-how-to-respond/)

**Verified evidence, the statutory hooks that make this a records problem.** Per the same source: Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17601(a)(2) requires five clear and conspicuous disclosures; § 17602(a)(1) requires them before fulfillment and in visual proximity to the consent request; § 17601(a)(3) defines "clearly and conspicuously" in terms of larger type, contrasting font or color, or attention-calling symbols; § 17602(d) requires an easy online cancellation mechanism; § 17602(a)(3) requires a retainable post-transaction acknowledgment. ARL violations can support CLRA claims under §§ 1770(a)(5) and (14), with courts recognizing that linkage (*King v. Bumble Trading, Inc.*, 393 F. Supp. 3d 856 (N.D. Cal. 2019)). CLRA § 1780 remedies include restitution, attorneys' fees, and punitive damages. The demand letter itself is the CLRA § 1782 thirty-day notice.

**Inference, from the statutory text.** Every one of those elements is a fact about a rendered page at a past moment. Type size, color contrast, and pixel proximity are not recoverable from a database record of "user accepted terms at timestamp T." That is the specific evidentiary hole.

**Verified evidence, consumer-side salience is enormous.** Hacker News threads: FTC announces click-to-cancel rule, 1,747 points and 760 comments ([item 41858665](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=41858665)); court nullifies the rule, 604 points and 559 comments ([item 44504699](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44504699)); California one-click cancellation law, 145 points ([item 41644315](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=41644315)).

**Verified evidence, and this one cuts against the idea.** The same Hacker News corpus shows almost nothing from the *operator* side. A query for "auto-renewal law" returns four stories total, the most relevant being a two-point submission from 2015. I found no thread of merchants discussing compliance burden, cost, or tooling. Reddit's search JSON endpoint refused my requests during this run, so I have **no** Reddit evidence either way and will not pretend otherwise.

**Assumption.** The absence of public operator complaints could mean the pain is handled quietly through counsel rather than discussed in public developer forums, or it could mean merchants do not feel it until a letter arrives. I cannot distinguish these from desk research, and the difference determines whether this is a business.

**Inference, from Churnkey's own product pages.** Churnkey solves the *cancellation* half of the statute, going forward, for customers large enough to be on the top tier. Their marketed scope does not include point-of-enrollment disclosure capture, and it does not include a retrospective evidence record. Those are the two things a demand letter attacks first.

**Churnkey open source.** [github.com/churnkey/sdk](https://github.com/churnkey/sdk), MIT licensed, created 2026-03-31, last push 2026-08-13, 21 stars, 0 forks, 0 open issues. A React cancel-flow component. Verified via GitHub API on 2026-08-25.

**Session replay tools** (Quantum Metric, FullStory, LogRocket and similar). These genuinely can reconstruct what a user saw and are the closest functional substitute.

**Verified evidence, and this is the important part.** Session replay is itself a live class-action target. Reported suits include *Doty et al. v. Quantum Metric, Inc.* (1:24-cv-01154), *Yoon v. Lululemon USA Inc.* naming Quantum Metric, plus filings against Bed Bath & Beyond and Lenovo, generally pleading state wiretapping and privacy theories. In the Lululemon matter a court held that disclosure of Quantum Metric's presence in the privacy policy did not constitute the consumer's consent. Sources: [Doty v. Quantum Metric complaint](https://www.classaction.org/media/doty-et-al-v-quantum-metric-inc.pdf), [Yoon v. Lululemon](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-dis-crt-cd-cal/2140803.html), [Law Street coverage](https://lawstreetmedia.com/news/tech/lululemon-quantum-metrics-motion-to-dismiss-wiretapping-suit-granted-in-part/).

**Inference.** Solving an ARL evidence problem by installing a tool that attracts CIPA and wiretapping claims is trading one class action for another. Any product here has to capture the *merchant's own rendered disclosure*, not the *consumer's inputs and keystrokes*, and has to say so loudly.

**The proven analog, in an adjacent regulated market.** ActiveProspect's TrustedForm does exactly this shape of thing for TCPA consent in lead generation. Verified from [activeprospect.com/trustedform/retain](https://activeprospect.com/trustedform/retain/) on 2026-08-25:

- Retain tiers: **$0.12 per first-party certificate** and **$0.15 per third-party certificate** up to 1,000/month, stepping down to $0.06 and $0.08 at volume.
- **$10/month minimum**, including 65 certificates, no annual commitment. Storage billed separately at **$0.0002 per certificate per month**.
- Certificates retained **up to 5 years**.
- Scale claims on the page: **2.5B+ leads certified per year**, **576M+ certificates stored per year**.
- Listed features include "Consent language detection" and "Event-based session replays."

**Inference, from TrustedForm's existence and pricing.** A consent-certificate business with per-record pricing is a proven, durable model in a US regulated market. It also shows the model tolerates session replay when scoped to the form event. That is a meaningful counterweight to the privacy risk above, though not a dismissal of it.

**Spreadsheets and counsel.** The realistic status quo is a one-time ARL audit by outside counsel, a set of checkout fixes, and no ongoing record. JMM describes exactly this: "Many businesses are now conducting ARL-specific website audits."

**Open source.** Essentially nothing. GitHub repository search on 2026-08-25 returned **1** result for "automatic renewal law" (0 stars, unrelated) and **4** for "auto-renewal compliance", all at 0 stars and none of them a compliance tool. The only relevant maintained project in the neighborhood is Churnkey's own cancel-flow SDK.

## 5. Wedge

The narrow entry point is **the enrollment moment, not the cancellation moment**, and **the historical record, not the live flow**.

Churnkey owns the cancel flow and gates state-aware compliance behind its top tier. Nobody found in this run sells a per-enrollment consent certificate for auto-renewal. The wedge is a single script tag that, at the instant of subscription consent, captures:

1. The rendered disclosure block as a static image plus its DOM subtree, scoped to the merchant's own disclosure and consent controls.
2. Computed geometry: pixel distance from disclosure to consent control, font size, and color contrast ratio. These map one-to-one onto the § 17601(a)(3) and § 17602(a)(1) tests.
3. A structured check of whether all five § 17601(a)(2) elements are present in the captured text.
4. Timestamp, consumer state derived from billing address, and a content hash.

Then, out of band: the post-transaction acknowledgment email that was sent, and every statutory renewal, trial-conversion, and price-increase notice that followed, on a per-state schedule.

The output is a certificate URL per subscriber and a one-click evidence packet per demand letter.

**Why a solo builder can win here specifically.** Churnkey's economic engine is saved revenue, so their roadmap points at retention features that increase saves. An evidence archive saves nobody. It is a cost center they can add but will not prioritize, and it is unattractive to a venture-scale company because it is a low-ARPU, high-obligation product. That is exactly the shape a solo operator can hold.

**Inference, not verified.** I am reasoning from Churnkey's public positioning and pricing structure, not from any statement of their roadmap.

## 6. MVP

Smallest thing that answers a demand letter:

1. **Capture script.** One script tag. On consent-control submit, serialize the merchant-designated disclosure region, rasterize it, compute geometry and contrast, hash, and POST. Explicitly does not capture keystrokes, form field values, or anything outside the designated region. Roughly two to three weeks for a strong developer using existing DOM-serialization libraries.
2. **Certificate store.** Append-only records, content-addressed, per-certificate share URL, retention set to the longest applicable statutory period. One to two weeks.
3. **Five-element linter.** Text classifier over the captured disclosure checking for the § 17601(a)(2) elements plus visual-proximity and contrast thresholds. One to two weeks.
4. **Notice scheduler.** Per-state rules table, subscriber state from billing address, scheduled sends via the merchant's existing email provider, logged into the same evidence store. Two to three weeks.
5. **Evidence packet export.** Given a subscriber ID or a date range, produce a PDF bundle. About one week.

**Inference, from the above.** Roughly eight to eleven focused weeks to a first paying install. The engineering is unremarkable. The genuinely hard part is not code, it is the per-state rules table, which requires legal review a solo developer cannot self-supply.

**Explicitly out of scope for v1:** cancel flows (Churnkey does that better), retention offers, and any claim about whether the merchant is compliant. The product records; it does not opine.

## 7. AI role

Narrow and real, in exactly one place: **classifying whether the captured disclosure text contains all five statutorily required elements**, and flagging drift when a merchant ships a checkout change that drops one. Disclosure language is free-form marketing prose, so a regex will not do it, and a language model reading a paragraph and returning "minimum purchase obligation: not found" is a good fit for the task.

**Everything else here is not AI work.** Geometry, contrast ratio, hashing, retention, and scheduling are deterministic and must be deterministic, because their output is meant to be evidence. Using a model where a computation belongs would actively weaken the product: a defensible record cannot rest on a probabilistic step.

**Inference.** Marketing this as an "AI compliance tool" would be a mistake. The value is that the record is boring, deterministic, and reproducible.

## 8. Pricing

Anchored to two verified points: TrustedForm at $0.12 to $0.15 per certificate with a $10/month floor, and Churnkey at $250/month for the sub-$5k-churn tier with state-aware compliance withheld until the Intelligence tier.

Proposed:

- **Record, $49/month**, includes 1,000 certificates/month, 12-month retention, five-element linter, evidence export. Sits far below Churnkey's entry price so it is not a competing purchase.
- **Defend, $199/month**, includes 10,000 certificates/month, retention to the longest applicable statutory period (California's three years, and one year after termination), per-state notice scheduling, and unlimited evidence packets.
- **Overage at $0.01 per certificate.** Deliberately an order of magnitude below TrustedForm, because a subscription enrollment certificate is a fraction of the cost to produce that a full lead-session certificate is, and because volume here is enrollments rather than leads.
- **Archive-only retention at $0.0005 per certificate per month** past the included window, mirroring TrustedForm's separate storage line.

**Inference on why this shape.** The buyer's alternative is not another product, it is doing nothing until a letter arrives. Priced under $200/month it clears a founder's discretionary threshold without a procurement conversation. Priced above that it competes with an actual legal budget and loses, because counsel is what they will buy instead.

**Assumption, unverified.** That merchants will pay anything at all in the absence of a live threat. This is the load-bearing assumption of the whole idea and nothing in this run tests it.

## 10. Risks

1. **Nobody buys insurance before the fire.** The evidence packet is worth a great deal on the day a letter arrives and appears worth nothing on every other day. Subscription pricing requires ongoing perceived value. This is the largest risk and it is not addressed by any evidence gathered here.
2. **The record can only start today.** A merchant sued over 2024 enrollments gets nothing from a tool installed in 2026. The product's value builds over years, which is the opposite of what a buyer under acute pressure wants. This may be fatal to the referral channel that is otherwise the best one.
3. **The product creates its own litigation surface.** Capturing what a consumer saw is adjacent to session replay, which is itself generating wiretapping class actions against Quantum Metric, Lululemon, Bed Bath & Beyond, and Lenovo. Careful scoping to the merchant's own rendered disclosure mitigates this. It does not eliminate the risk of being named alongside a customer.
4. **A solo developer cannot maintain the legal rules table.** Nine jurisdictions changed rules between July 2025 and January 2027, per the two law-firm trackers above. Keeping a per-state notice schedule accurate is ongoing legal work, and an inaccurate schedule is worse than no schedule because it manufactures a false compliance record. This forces either a paid legal advisor or an explicit disclaimer that weakens the pitch.
5. **Churnkey moves down-market.** They already ship state-aware compliance. Adding enrollment-side capture is a quarter of work for a funded team that already sits in the checkout path.
6. **Stripe or the billing platform absorbs it.** Whoever owns the billing object is the natural home for a consent record attached to a subscription.
7. **Evidentiary weight is unproven.** A certificate is only useful if counsel believes a court would credit it. TrustedForm has years of precedent behind it in TCPA matters. A new product has none, and "our vendor says this is what the page looked like" is a weaker position than it sounds until it has survived a challenge.
8. **Louisiana's small-business exemption points the wrong way.** Businesses under 50 employees or $5 million revenue are broadly exempt there. If that drafting pattern spreads, the small merchants a solo builder can reach self-serve are precisely the ones with no obligation, while the obligated merchants buy from vendors with SOC 2 reports and legal indemnities.
9. **Market size is unmodeled.** I could not obtain a defensible count of US online subscription merchants in this run. Recharge cites 20,000+ merchants on its own platform, which is one platform's share, not a market. Without that number the addressable market is a guess.

## 11. Kill criteria

Falsifiable, thresholded, ordered so the cheapest and most likely killer runs first. The first two need no code.

1. **Pre-incident willingness test.** Interview 20 subscription merchants who have **not** received a demand letter. If fewer than 5 say they would pay $49/month today for an enrollment evidence record, **stop**. This directly tests risk 1, which is the one most likely to kill the idea, and it is answerable in three weeks.
2. **Counsel referral test.** Ask 10 firms that publish ARL demand-letter guidance whether they would recommend such a tool to clients. If fewer than 4 say yes, **stop**. The best channel is closed and nothing else found here replaces it.
3. **Retroactivity test.** Ask those same firms whether a record starting at install date is useful when the class period predates it. If a majority say it is not, **stop** or pivot to a pure notice-scheduling product, because risk 2 has been confirmed as fatal to the pitch.
4. **Linter yield test.** Run the free checkout audit against 200 randomly sampled US subscription checkouts. If fewer than 60 fail at least one of the five-element, visual-proximity, or contrast checks, **stop**. Under a 30% failure rate the audit generates too few qualified leads to seed the channel.
5. **Conversion test.** Of merchants shown a specific failing finding on their own checkout, if fewer than 3% start a paid trial within 30 days, **stop**. The channel does not convert.
6. **Incumbent test.** If Churnkey, Stripe, or Recharge ships enrollment-side consent capture before 25 paying merchants are on board, **stop** and do not try to out-feature them.
7. **Retention test.** If more than 40% of paying merchants churn within 120 days of first invoice, **stop**. It confirms the product is a one-time reassurance purchase rather than a subscription.

## 12. Verdict: Watch

**Watch**, and specifically watch by running kill criteria 1 and 2 before writing any code.

The reasoning that drove it:

**What argues for Build.** The trend is not a guess. Nine jurisdictions have dated obligations landing between July 2025 and January 2027, from two independent law-firm trackers. The federal rule is being rebuilt after vacatur, and the FTC extracted $2.5 billion from Amazon in the middle of the vacatur, so enforcement appetite is not theoretical. The pain is documented in a defense firm's own words, with an actual demand letter reproduced. The wedge is genuinely open: the nearest incumbent gates state-aware compliance to its top tier and addresses the cancellation half of the statute only. Open source is absent. And the business model has a proven analog in TrustedForm, which sells almost exactly this shape of product in an adjacent US regulated market at per-record pricing.

**What holds it back, and it is decisive.** Every piece of pain evidence I gathered comes from lawyers describing what happens *after* a merchant is sued. Not one comes from a merchant saying they want to prevent it. That asymmetry is the whole problem. A subscription product needs a buyer who feels the need monthly, and the evidence supports a buyer who feels it once, acutely, at a moment when the product can no longer help them because the record starts at install. Risks 1 and 2 compound rather than stack: the moment of maximum willingness to pay is exactly the moment the product has the least to offer.

Two secondary considerations reinforce Watch rather than Build. The Hacker News corpus shows enormous consumer interest and near-zero merchant discussion of compliance burden, and Reddit was unreachable in this run, so I have no direct voice-of-customer evidence at all. And Louisiana's exemption for businesses under 50 employees or $5 million revenue suggests the legislative drafting trend may carve out precisely the segment a solo builder can reach self-serve.

Watch is the honest call. The demand-side question is cheap to answer and has not been answered. Kill criterion 1 costs three weeks of interviews and zero code, and it will settle this. If merchants who have never been sued will pay, this becomes a Build quickly, because everything else about it already checks out.
