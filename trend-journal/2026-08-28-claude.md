# ScriptLedger: PCI DSS payment page script evidence for small and mid US e-commerce merchants (2026-08-28)

**Contributor:** Claude (Cowork scheduled task)
**File:** `trend-journal/2026-08-28-claude.md`
**Market:** United States first. The standard itself is global, but the buying motion runs through US acquirers, US ISOs, and US QSA firms. Indonesia relevance: the standard applies there too, but merchant compliance enforcement is driven by the acquirer relationship, and the acquirer economics that fund this purchase are not established outside the US and EU.
**Motion:** B2B. An e-commerce merchant pays out of an engineering or compliance budget. A developer or a solo technical owner installs it. The QSA, the ISO, or the acquirer is the party demanding the evidence, and is the most plausible acquisition intermediary. None of those channels are validated.
**Verdict:** **Watch**

## Summary

PCI DSS v4.0.1 Requirements 6.4.3 and 11.6.1 force e-commerce merchants to maintain an authorized inventory of every script on a payment page, justify each one in writing, verify script integrity, and run a mechanism that alerts on unauthorized changes to payment page scripts and security-impacting HTTP headers as received by the consumer browser. Those requirements became mandatory on 31 March 2025. Seventeen months later the market has not settled, because the PCI Security Standards Council pulled 6.4.3 and 11.6.1 out of the shortest self-assessment form (SAQ A) five weeks before the deadline and replaced them with one vague eligibility sentence, then published guidance saying the way to satisfy that sentence is to implement 6.4.3 and 11.6.1 anyway.

The pain is real, sourced from named practitioners rather than vendor marketing, and recurring on an annual attestation cycle. The problem is the supply side. At least eighteen commercial products already target these two requirement numbers directly, entry pricing is USD 0 to USD 99 per month, and the CDN and WAF incumbents bundle it. The thing that actually closes a sale here is assessor acceptance, which is relationship and validation capital that a solo builder does not start with. Watch rather than Build, with a short and falsifiable test list.

## 1. Trend durability

**Verified evidence.** PCI DSS v4.0 was published 31 March 2022. Requirements 6.4.3 and 11.6.1 were future dated and became mandatory for all applicable merchants and service providers on 31 March 2025 ([TrustedSec, 4 March 2025](https://trustedsec.com/blog/the-hidden-trap-in-the-pci-dss-saq-a-changes); [Scott Helme, 13 March 2025](https://scotthelme.co.uk/pci-dss-faq-saq-wtf-bbq/)).

**Verified evidence.** On the last day of January 2025, roughly two months before the deadline, the Council published SAQ A r1, which removed Requirements 6.4.3, 11.6.1, and 12.3.1 and added a single eligibility sentence in their place: "The merchant has confirmed that their site is not susceptible to attacks from scripts that could affect the merchant's e-commerce system(s)." The new SAQ A took effect 1 April 2025 ([Scott Helme](https://scotthelme.co.uk/pci-dss-faq-saq-wtf-bbq/); [TrustedSec](https://trustedsec.com/blog/the-hidden-trap-in-the-pci-dss-saq-a-changes)).

**Verified evidence.** On 28 February 2025 the Council published FAQ 1588, which says a merchant can satisfy that eligibility sentence either by using techniques such as those detailed in Requirements 6.4.3 and 11.6.1, or by obtaining confirmation from its PCI DSS compliant third-party service provider or payment processor that the provider's embedded payment form includes protections against script attacks. The criterion applies only to merchants embedding a provider's payment page or form, for example in an iframe. It does not apply to redirect flows or fully outsourced sites ([PCI SSC blog, 28 February 2025](https://blog.pcisecuritystandards.org/faq-clarifies-new-saq-a-eligibility-criteria-for-e-commerce-merchants)).

**Verified evidence.** The Council published an information supplement, "Guidance for PCI DSS Requirements 6.4.3 and 11.6.1", on 10 March 2025, twenty-one days before the deadline ([TrustedSec](https://trustedsec.com/blog/the-hidden-trap-in-the-pci-dss-saq-a-changes); copy hosted by [Scott Helme](https://scotthelme.co.uk/pci-dss-faq-saq-wtf-bbq/)).

**Verified evidence, and this is the durability signal.** The confusion is still live in mid 2026. Jeff Hall, a QSA writing as PCI Guru, reports that the Council's May 2026 Assessor Newsletter made FAQ 1588 its FAQ of the Month because of "the significant number of questions on the Q1 All Assessor Session in March" 2026 about how SAQ A merchants demonstrate their sites are not affected by scripts, and that the newsletter republished the FAQ without answering the question ([PCI Guru, 10 June 2026](https://pciguru.wordpress.com/2026/06/10/saq-a-rears-its-ugly-head-again/)).

**Inference, from the 2025 primary sources plus the June 2026 assessor commentary.** This is a lasting shift and not a spike. The controls are permanent line items in SAQ A-EP, SAQ D, and the Report on Compliance template, they must be re-evidenced at every annual attestation, and the one ambiguity that could have reduced the burden has instead generated fifteen months of continuing assessor questions. Demand is structurally recurring.

**Inference, from the same sources.** The demand *event* nevertheless peaked in Q1 2025. A product entering in late 2026 arrives after the scramble, into a market where most affected merchants have already picked something.

**Assumption, unverified.** That PCI DSS v5.0, which PCI Guru was already writing about in March 2026, does not materially rewrite or relax these two requirements. I did not research the v5.0 timeline or content and it could invalidate this whole analysis.

**Google Trends: not obtained.** The research environment available to this scheduled run could not reach `trends.google.com`, and web search did not return Trends series data. I am not going to invent an interest-over-time curve. The durability claim above rests on the dated primary and practitioner sources, not on search volume, and that is a real gap in this entry.

## 2. Customer and JTBD

**Inference, from the scope rules in the PCI SSC FAQ and the QSA commentary.** The buyer is a US e-commerce merchant that hosts its own storefront and invokes payment from it, in one of two situations:

- The merchant validates on SAQ A-EP or SAQ D, where 6.4.3 and 11.6.1 apply in full as written line items.
- The merchant validates on SAQ A with an embedded iframe form, where the two requirements were formally removed but the eligibility sentence pushes most merchants back toward implementing them anyway.

**Verified evidence.** SAQ A is the shortest form, with roughly 21 controls falling to 19 after the revision. SAQ A-EP carries 128 requirements for e-commerce sites. Losing SAQ A eligibility is therefore a cliff, not a step ([TrustedSec](https://trustedsec.com/blog/the-hidden-trap-in-the-pci-dss-saq-a-changes)).

**Inference.** The job being hired for is not "secure my checkout." It is "give me a document my QSA, my acquirer, or my own signature on an attestation will accept, without me having to become an expert in Content Security Policy." The security outcome is a byproduct. The artifact is the product.

**Assumption, unverified.** That the person who feels this is a technical founder or a lone in-house developer rather than a compliance manager with a purchasing process. I did not interview anyone.

## 3. Pain evidence

**Verified evidence, non-vendor, named practitioners.**

- Chris Camejo, QSA at TrustedSec: "As a QSA, we and many of our clients are confused and frustrated by this change to SAQ A only a few weeks before the future-dated requirement deadline... it's hard to understand why PCI SSC would remove requirements 6.4.3 and 11.6.1 from the SAQ only to then recommend implementing requirements 6.4.3 and 11.6.1 as a way to meet the new eligibility criterion." He also warns that merchants "may be surprised at the start of an assessment or after a breach when a QSA or forensic investigator informs the merchant that they are not eligible for SAQ A" ([TrustedSec, 4 March 2025](https://trustedsec.com/blog/the-hidden-trap-in-the-pci-dss-saq-a-changes)).
- Scott Helme, describing his own customer load at the time of the change: "This is when customers started reaching out en masse to understand what this change meant" and "I was fielding countless questions from confused customers." On the processor attestation route he wrote: "Despite scouring the documentation of the TPSP's I'm most familiar with, I've not yet found anything that could reasonably be considered a 'confirmation' from the TPSP that their solution satisfies this new SAQ A eligibility criteria" ([Scott Helme, 13 March 2025](https://scotthelme.co.uk/pci-dss-faq-saq-wtf-bbq/)).
- Jeff Hall, QSA: "What drove most QSAs over the edge with this FAQ is that first bullet. If 6.4.3 and 11.6.1 are still relevant, why were they then removed from SAQ A? The assessor community has never really been given a straight answer to that question." On the processor route, fifteen months after Helme: "what I have seen in Responsibility Matrices from almost all service providers is 'weaselly' explanations on how they cover script management, particularly those providing iFrame solutions where they almost all recommend that the merchant be using their own methods to cover their website" ([PCI Guru, 10 June 2026](https://pciguru.wordpress.com/2026/06/10/saq-a-rears-its-ugly-head-again/)).
- A commenter on that post, 11 June 2026: "Still can't believe we're 2+ years into this and PCI DSS remains deliberately vague" and "the FAQs, guidance documents, SAQs, and blog posts actively contradict each other. Pick a lane" ([PCI Guru comments](https://pciguru.wordpress.com/2026/06/10/saq-a-rears-its-ugly-head-again/)).

**Verified evidence that the processor escape hatch is narrower than it reads.** Adyen's own documentation tells SAQ A merchants that "as a SAQ A merchant you must ensure that the payment page elements and scripts that are loaded from your providers through different integrations are PCI DSS compliant, and apply security measures to protect from script attacks," and offers its annual Attestation of Compliance as help rather than as a blanket confirmation ([Adyen Docs, SAQ A eligibility](https://docs.adyen.com/development-resources/pci-dss-compliance-guide/saq-a-eligibility)).

**Gap I could not close.** I did not find first-person merchant complaints, only expert and consultant accounts of merchant confusion. Attempts to reach Reddit search were blocked in this environment and site-scoped searches returned no threads. The pain is well attested by people who sell to or assess merchants, which is one step removed from the buyer's own words. That matters and is reflected in the verdict.

## 4. Alternatives

**Verified evidence, named products aimed explicitly at 6.4.3 and 11.6.1.** PylonSec, Imperva Client-Side Protection, Feroot, DataDome Page Protect, SecurityMetrics Shopping Cart Monitor, Foregenix File Integrity Monitoring, Source Defense (free tier for a single page), Visualping, CHEQ, Akamai Client-Side Protection ([awesome-pci-dss](https://github.com/junhui/awesome-pci-dss)). Add cside, Jscrambler, Reflectiz, Cloudflare, HUMAN Security, otto-js, F5 Client-Side Defense, DataStealth, and Sift, all of which cside itself maintains head-to-head comparison pages against ([cside compare index](https://cside.com/compare)). Add Report URI's Script Watch and Policy Watch ([Scott Helme](https://scotthelme.co.uk/pci-dss-faq-saq-wtf-bbq/)).

**Verified evidence, pricing.** cside publishes a free tier (1,000 API calls per month, no credit card; a separate signup CTA cites 2,000 page views) and a paid plan from USD 99 per month, and states that its methodology was validated by VikingCloud for Requirements 6.4.3 and 11.6.1 ([cside, PCI DSS compliance cost, updated 19 July 2026](https://cside.com/blog/pci-dss-compliance-cost)).

**Vendor claim, not independent.** The same cside page asserts that manual compliance costs 15 to 30 engineering days per year and that Level 4 merchants spend USD 5,000 to 25,000 per year on PCI overall, attributing the merchant-level ranges to PCI SSC and Verizon benchmarks. I did not verify those against the underlying reports and they are a vendor's marketing figures. Treat them as directional at best.

**Verified evidence, the do-it-yourself alternative is cheap and legitimate.** TrustedSec, writing as a QSA firm, describes Content Security Policy as "a key tool to confirm that scripts are authorized and have integrity", points to Subresource Integrity for third and fourth party scripts, and says of 11.6.1: "A simple script that periodically loads the page and checks specific header, CSP, and SRI parameters could be sufficient to meet this requirement. The reporting mechanism built into CSP can also be used" ([TrustedSec](https://trustedsec.com/blog/the-hidden-trap-in-the-pci-dss-saq-a-changes)).

**Inference.** The floor competitor is not another startup. It is a cron job, a CSP report endpoint, and a spreadsheet, blessed by the merchant's own QSA. That caps price hard at the small end.

**Verified evidence, open source is thin here.** The one curated list I found (`junhui/awesome-pci-dss`, 17 stars, 1 commit) lists no open source project under its 6.4.3 and 11.6.1 section, only commercial products; its open source section covers Wazuh, OpenControl, Vault, OpenVAS, OSSEC, none of which address payment page script inventory ([awesome-pci-dss](https://github.com/junhui/awesome-pci-dss)).

**Inference.** The absence of an obvious open source incumbent is the one genuinely encouraging supply-side fact in this entry. It is outweighed by the count of funded commercial entrants and by the CSP and SRI browser standards themselves being free and sufficient.

## 5. Wedge

**Inference, from the alternatives above.** The generic "monitor scripts on payment pages" product is taken. Three narrower entry points survive that reasoning, and all three are hypotheses rather than findings:

1. **The evidence packet, not the monitor.** Most listed products sell detection. The artifact a self-assessing SAQ A merchant actually lacks is a dated, signable document that records the eligibility determination: which route under FAQ 1588 was taken, the script inventory with written justification per script, the integrity method, the alert mechanism and its cadence, and the processor's responsibility matrix language pasted in. Sell the file, monitor only enough to populate it.
2. **Platform-native distribution.** A one-click app inside a storefront platform's own marketplace, where the install is a template edit rather than a DNS or tag manager change, and where the platform's app store does the discovery.
3. **Channel through the assessor.** A white-label evidence generator sold to small QSA firms, ISOs, and ASVs who currently hand merchants a spreadsheet, priced per merchant seat.

**Assumption, unverified and load bearing.** That any of these is unserved. cside deploys with one script tag and no DNS change and already exports a QSA report in seconds, which erodes wedge 1 and wedge 2 substantially.

## 6. MVP

**Inference, from the control text quoted by TrustedSec and the PCI SSC guidance.** Smallest shippable scope for one strong developer:

- A first-party JavaScript beacon on the payment page that reports every executing script's source URL and a hash of its contents as delivered to the browser.
- A CSP `report-to` collector endpoint, so the merchant gets both the allowlist enforcement and the violation stream.
- A weekly headless browser fetch of the payment page that captures security-impacting response headers and the script set, diffs against the last approved baseline, and emails on change. Weekly is the floor the requirement names.
- An approval queue where a human marks each new script authorized, with a free-text business justification field.
- A PDF export containing the inventory, the justifications, the integrity method per script, the alert configuration, the change log, and the eligibility determination.
- Stripe Billing for subscriptions.

**Inference on effort.** Four to eight focused weeks to a usable v1 for a developer already comfortable with headless browsers and CSP. The hard parts are not the code. They are handling third-party scripts that legitimately change and invalidate hashes, a problem TrustedSec names explicitly, and getting even one QSA to say the output is acceptable.

## 7. AI role

**Stated plainly: AI adds little here, and claiming otherwise would be dishonest.** The control is a diff against an approved baseline. A hash comparison is exact, auditable, and explainable to an assessor. A model that probabilistically judges whether a script changed meaningfully is strictly worse for this job, because the buyer needs determinism they can defend in an audit.

**Inference, the only defensible narrow use.** Drafting the written business justification per script, which 6.4.3 requires in prose and which merchants hate writing, by classifying a known script domain (analytics, chat, tag manager, payment) and proposing a sentence for a human to approve. That is a convenience feature worth maybe a checkbox on a pricing page. It is not a reason to build the company, and it would not satisfy this repo's bar that AI must serve a specific workflow advantage.

## 8. Pricing

**Inference, anchored to the one published competitor price point.** cside's free tier and USD 99 per month paid tier set the ceiling for an undifferentiated entrant. A solo product cannot price above a funded competitor with QSA validation while offering less.

- Free: one payment page, weekly check, inventory view, no export.
- USD 29 per month: one site, weekly checks, alerting, PDF evidence export. Priced below the incumbent because the offer is narrower.
- USD 79 per month: multiple sites or environments, CSP report collection, change history retention for a full attestation year.
- Channel tier, per merchant seat, negotiated: white label for a QSA or ISO managing a merchant book.

**Assumption, unverified.** That a merchant already receiving a free tier from cside would pay USD 29 for a better evidence document. This is the single most important untested assumption in the entry and I have no evidence for it.

## 9. Distribution

**Inference.** The first 100 customers would have to come from a channel that the incumbents' SEO does not already own, because they own it comprehensively. cside alone publishes head-to-head comparison pages against at least ten named competitors plus cost, checklist, and "how to comply" guides. Competing on search terms containing "6.4.3" is a losing move for a solo builder.

Plausible channels, none validated:

- Storefront platform app marketplaces, where discovery is inside the merchant's admin rather than on Google.
- Small QSA and ASV firms and regional ISOs, reached one at a time, who need something to hand merchants and would rather not build it.
- The practitioner community that is visibly still arguing about this: the PCI Guru comment threads, r/pcicompliance, and the Security Stack Exchange `pci-dss` tag, both listed as the community venues in [awesome-pci-dss](https://github.com/junhui/awesome-pci-dss). Useful for learning, unproven for acquisition.

**Assumption, unverified.** That a solo builder can get a QSA firm to endorse or resell an unvalidated tool. Nothing in the research supports this and cside's route (a formal VikingCloud validation) suggests the opposite: endorsement is bought with an assessment engagement.

## 10. Risks

1. **The market is already contested by well-funded specialists and by platform incumbents.** Eighteen-plus named products, at least two free tiers, and Cloudflare, Akamai, Imperva, and F5 bundling it into infrastructure the merchant already buys. Verified from the vendor and list sources above.
2. **The buyer's cheapest compliant path may be free.** FAQ 1588's second bullet lets a merchant lean on the processor. If processors move from "weaselly" responsibility matrices toward clean written confirmations, the SAQ A segment stops buying anything at all. Helme found no such confirmation in March 2025 and Hall found only weak ones in June 2026, so this has not happened yet, but it is one product decision away at Stripe or Adyen.
3. **The technical core is a commodity that a QSA will tell the merchant to build themselves.** TrustedSec says so in writing.
4. **Assessor acceptance is the real product, and it is not code.** A merchant will not buy an evidence tool their QSA has never heard of.
5. **Liability tail.** Selling an artifact whose purpose is to be relied on in an audit or after a breach means a solo operator is holding risk that is disproportionate to a USD 29 subscription.
6. **Standard risk.** PCI DSS v5.0 is already being discussed. The requirement numbering and scope could shift under the product.

## 11. Kill criteria

Falsifiable, with thresholds, ordered cheapest first.

- **Channel test.** Contact 25 small QSA, ASV, and ISO firms with a one-page description of the white-label evidence generator. Kill if fewer than 3 agree to a 30 minute call within 3 weeks, or if fewer than 1 will name a price they would pay per merchant.
- **Processor path test.** Read the current published responsibility matrix or script-security guidance for Stripe, Adyen, Braintree, and Shopify Payments. Kill if 2 or more now publish a clean written confirmation that a merchant can hand to a QSA to satisfy the SAQ A eligibility criterion, because the largest merchant segment then has a free compliant path.
- **Willingness to pay test.** Put a landing page in front of 300 qualified merchant visitors offering the USD 29 evidence tier. Kill if fewer than 5 reach a checkout page, or if fewer than 2 pay for a first month.
- **Incumbent free-tier test.** Sign up for cside's free tier and Source Defense's single-page free tier and generate an evidence export for a test payment page. Kill if either output would plausibly satisfy a QSA on its own for a single-site merchant, since the proposed wedge then does not exist.
- **Assessor acceptance test.** Get 3 independent QSAs to review a sample evidence PDF. Kill if 2 or more say they would not accept it without a formal vendor validation.
- **Standard-risk gate.** Kill immediately if PCI SSC publishes a v5.0 draft that removes, merges, or materially rewrites 6.4.3 or 11.6.1.

## 12. Verdict: Watch

**Reasoning.**

What earns Watch rather than Reject: the pain is documented by three named, credible, non-vendor practitioners across an eighteen-month span, the most recent in June 2026, and the confusion is visibly unresolved rather than fading. The obligation recurs at every annual attestation, which is subscription shaped. The processor escape hatch that would have killed demand outright has, on the evidence of both Helme in 2025 and Hall in 2026, not actually materialized. And the specific artifact merchants need, a defensible evidence document rather than a monitoring dashboard, is not obviously what the incumbents sell.

What prevents Build: every fact I gathered about the supply side points the wrong way. Eighteen-plus named competitors, free tiers at USD 0, a funded specialist at USD 99 per month with a formal QSA validation and a dominant content position, four infrastructure incumbents bundling the capability, and a QSA firm publicly telling merchants the control can be met with a simple periodic script. The differentiator that would matter, assessor acceptance, is precisely the asset a solo builder cannot bootstrap. On top of that, my pain evidence is entirely second-hand: consultants describing confused merchants, not merchants speaking for themselves. The repo's bar for promotion is real sourced pain plus a durable trend plus a clear wedge. This clears the first two and fails the third.

**What would change the verdict to Build.** The channel test passing decisively, meaning three or more small assessor or ISO firms saying they would resell a white-label evidence generator and naming a per-merchant price, combined with the incumbent free-tier test showing that existing free outputs do not stand alone as audit evidence. Absent both, this stays in the journal.

**What would change it to Reject.** Any two of: processors publishing clean confirmations, cside or an equivalent shipping a genuinely sufficient free evidence export, or a PCI DSS v5.0 draft that rewrites these requirements.
