# AwardLedger: per-student ESA and voucher funding reconciliation for small US private schools (2026-08-31)

**Contributor:** Claude (Cowork scheduled task)
**File:** `trend-journal/2026-08-31-claude.md`
**Market:** United States first. The mechanism is US state school-choice statute plus state-contracted scholarship intermediaries. Indonesia relevance: not established, and the mechanism does not transfer.
**Motion:** B2B. A participating private school pays, out of a business-office or bookkeeping budget. The school administrator, business manager, or head of school uses it. Parents are inside the workflow as approvers but never as buyers. Private school associations, diocesan offices, and school-choice advocacy networks are the plausible acquisition intermediaries, none of them validated.
**Verdict:** **Watch**

---

## Summary

Two of the largest state education savings account programs in the country now pay private schools through the same three-step mechanic: the school submits a per-student, per-quarter invoice into a state-contracted portal, a parent has to log in and approve it, and only then does money move. Florida has run this design since HB1 turned its scholarships into ESAs, and it has visibly broken: a state audit, a $47 million funding shortfall, roughly 27,000 double-counted students in a single quarter, legislative testimony from a school head who says her school is still owed $50,000, and a February 2026 lawsuit by seven private schools. Texas launched the identical mechanic this month, with roughly 800 participating schools going through it for the first time.

**AwardLedger** would be a shadow receivables ledger for the school side of that flow. It would hold the school's own roster and tuition agreements, ingest whatever payment and billing exports the state portal gives the school, and reconcile them per student, per quarter, so the school can see at any moment which students are funded, which invoices are sitting unapproved by a parent, which quarters were paid short, and what the school is actually owed.

The demand evidence here is the strongest I have logged in this journal. The verdict is Watch anyway, and for four supply-side reasons rather than any doubt about the pain. First, the entire product depends on a machine-readable export from portals I could not verify offer one. Second, Step Up For Students states on its own site that using third-party services to access accounts violates its user agreement, which closes the automation path. Third, the product is a flashlight and not a fix: it tells a school what it is owed, it does not make the state pay, and no evidence I found shows schools paying money for visibility alone. Fourth, both states are actively legislating and re-engineering the exact failure the product would monetize. Every one of those four is cheap to test with two weeks of phone calls before a line of code is written.

## 1. Trend durability

**Verified evidence**

- Florida's scholarship programs are large and the state's own funding machinery could not keep up with them. In October 2024 the state census found roughly 23,000 students counted by both a school district and a scholarship funding organization, and the state froze their scholarship funds. By spring 2025 the state was $47 million short on the fourth quarterly payment to districts. ([Jacksonville Today, 2026-02-22](https://jaxtoday.org/2026/02/22/florida-private-schools-sue-step-up-for-students-over-voucher-payout-problems/))
- The problem got worse, not better, in the following year. In the first quarter of 2025-26, more than 27,000 students were "matched" as both a scholarship student and a public school student, and at least $7 million went out to families before that check ran. ([Florida Phoenix, 2025-10-22](https://floridaphoenix.com/2025/10/22/school-daze-rapid-expansion-of-voucher-program-leaves-public-private-schools-underpaid/))
- Step Up For Students received scholarship applications from more than 500,000 students last year and its recent tax filings show annual revenue around $1 billion. On 2026-02-20 the state Board of Education renewed Step Up and AAA Scholarship Foundation as funding organizations and approved a third, Sunshine State Kids Foundation. ([Jacksonville Today](https://jaxtoday.org/2026/02/22/florida-private-schools-sue-step-up-for-students-over-voucher-payout-problems/))
- Texas is standing up the same structure right now. On 2025-10-06 the Comptroller named the program Texas Education Freedom Accounts and selected Odyssey as the certified educational assistance organization, for a launch at the beginning of the 2026-27 school year. Odyssey's platform is described as supporting "parents, schools, service providers and state administrators" and Odyssey is named as already operating in Iowa, Georgia, Louisiana, Utah, and Wyoming. ([Texas Comptroller press release, 2025-10-06](https://comptroller.texas.gov/about/media-center/news/20251006-texas-comptroller-announces-texas-education-freedom-accounts-as-program-name-for-school-choice-program-selects-implementation-partner-1759758132101))
- The Texas program is still issuing awards in waves off a waitlist as of this month, with separate announcements of roughly 4,500 and then nearly 15,000 additional Tier 3 awards. ([TEFA news index](https://educationfreedom.texas.gov/newsupdates/), linked from the [tuition and fee guidelines page](https://educationfreedom.texas.gov/newsupdates/tuition-and-fee-guidelines-for-participating-private-schools/), page last modified 2026-08-24)

**Reported, not independently verified** (search-result summaries of pages I did not open)

- Nearly 800 Texas private schools signed up to accept the new money, per a Comptroller map (Fox 7 Austin). At least 257,000 students applied against a 90,000-student cap, most accepted families receiving about $10,300, with a $1 billion ceiling for the biennium ending 2027-08-31 (Community Impact, Texas Tribune).
- Odyssey was founded in 2021 and has won contracts in eight states; its Texas contract lets the Comptroller impose financial penalties for platform downtime or slow transaction processing (Houston Chronicle).
- Florida's FTC and FES-EO programs together serve 286,284 students, and average total enrollment at a participating school is 155 students (EdChoice).

**Inference**

This is a structural shift, not a spike. Twenty-five years of Florida program history, a universal-eligibility expansion in 2023, a second mega-state launching in 2026-27, a single vendor operating in eight states, and a federal tax-credit scholarship on the near horizon all point the same direction. The volume of money routed to private schools through per-student state intermediaries is going up and will keep going up for at least several school years.

**The important qualifier, and it cuts against the idea.** The durability of the *market* is not the same as the durability of the *gap*. Florida is actively trying to fix exactly the failure this product would monetize. The Legislature is debating cutting the funding organizations' administrative take from 3% to 2%, Senate Bill 318 aimed at separating voucher funds from public school funds passed the Senate unanimously, the Department of Education is building cross-check systems, and two representatives on the budget panel proposed tying payments to Florida Education Identifier numbers ([Jacksonville Today](https://jaxtoday.org/2026/02/22/florida-private-schools-sue-step-up-for-students-over-voucher-payout-problems/); [Florida Phoenix](https://floridaphoenix.com/2025/10/22/school-daze-rapid-expansion-of-voucher-program-leaves-public-private-schools-underpaid/)). A product whose pitch is "the state's payment system is a mess" is betting against a legislature that has already started fixing it.

## 2. Customer and job to be done

**Motion:** B2B. **Who pays:** the participating private school. **Who uses:** the business manager, bookkeeper, or head of school, which at this school size is frequently the same person. **Who is in the workflow but never pays:** the parent, who must click approve, and the scholarship funding organization, which is the counterparty.

**Verified evidence on the buyer's job.** Keva Hampton, head of Inner City Christian School in Jacksonville, told the Florida House PreK-12 Budget Subcommittee that her school and others experience "chronic payment delays, fluctuating scholarship amounts, technical failures, lack of consistent communication, and we can go on." She said her school was still owed $50,000 in state dollars from the previous school year. ([Florida Phoenix, 2025-10-22](https://floridaphoenix.com/2025/10/22/school-daze-rapid-expansion-of-voucher-program-leaves-public-private-schools-underpaid/))

**Inference on the JTBD.** The job is not "reduce data entry." The job is: *tell me, today, how much of this year's tuition is actually going to arrive, from whom, and what I have to chase to get the rest.* A school that cannot answer that cannot make payroll decisions, cannot decide whether to renew a teacher contract, and cannot tell a family whether it owes a balance. Hampton's testimony is a direct statement that schools cannot currently answer it.

**Assumption.** That the person feeling this pain has budget authority and is not already fully absorbed by a business office that has simply accepted the chaos as the cost of participating. Not verified.

## 3. Pain evidence

**Verified evidence**

- **The administrator's own documentation concedes the confusion.** Step Up For Students published a page addressed to partner schools that opens: "We understand that there may be some confusion when you see your second and third quarter's student invoices." It then walks through a worked example in which a student with $11,000 of tuition and a $10,000 award generates two separate invoices in each of quarters two, three, and four, because unpaid tuition from the prior quarter is carried forward and invoiced separately. The page ends with the instruction, "If a student has two invoices, please approve both, and they will both be paid." ([Step Up For Students, school important message](https://www.stepupforstudents.org/school-important-message/))
- **Payment is gated on a parent clicking a button.** "Each quarter, your student's school will initiate the scholarship payment process by submitting an invoice for you to approve in your EMA account. Before funds can be released, a parent or guardian must log in to EMA to review and either approve or deny the payment." Parents have thirty calendar days to approve. ([Step Up For Students, PS Fund](https://www.stepupforstudents.org/scholarships/private-school/fund/))
- **There is a long structural lag before money even reaches the intermediary.** For 2026-27 the published schedule runs enrollment deadline to funds delivered as June 15 to August 1, July 15 to September 1, September 15 to November 1, December 15 to February 1, and February 15 to April 1. ([Step Up For Students, PS Fund](https://www.stepupforstudents.org/scholarships/private-school/fund/))
- **There are silent per-student failure modes that zero out funding.** A notice dated 2026-08-18 states that for students whose Public School Attestation is not submitted, "Funds for students whose attestation is not submitted will be returned to their funding source." ([Step Up For Students site notice](https://www.stepupforstudents.org/scholarships/private-school/fund/))
- **Texas creates a clawback the school must track per student.** If a student stops participating in TEFA, "the school will be expected to return any tuition and fees paid in advance to the state." ([TEFA tuition and fee guidelines](https://educationfreedom.texas.gov/newsupdates/tuition-and-fee-guidelines-for-participating-private-schools/))
- **Schools escalated to litigation.** On 2026-02-20 seven private schools sued Step Up For Students in Duval County. Their attorney, Lamonte Carter, put the loss at "$500,000 to a million" per school in unpaid scholarships and related indirect expenses, and said the difficulty receiving correct payments dates back five years. Step Up's spokesperson responded that the claims are specific to those schools and not reflective of the program as a whole. ([Jacksonville Today, 2026-02-22](https://jaxtoday.org/2026/02/22/florida-private-schools-sue-step-up-for-students-over-voucher-payout-problems/))

**Reported, not independently verified.** WLRN, WUSF, and the Tampa Bay Times reported the same suit, with delays described as ranging from 90 days to over a year, partial payments, unexplained funding reductions, and in some cases no payments for more than two years, seeking damages exceeding $50,000.

**Honest weighting of this evidence.** The attorney's "$500,000 to a million" figure is a plaintiff's litigation claim and should be read as an allegation, not a measured loss. Hampton's $50,000 is a self-reported figure in legislative testimony. The strongest items are the ones I would not have expected the vendor to publish about itself: the Step Up page conceding invoice confusion, and the thirty-day parent approval gate. Those are first-party and unambiguous.

**What I could not find, and it matters.** I found no buyer-voiced complaint in an open forum. Searches for school administrators discussing EMA invoice chasing returned only official documentation and SEO content. Reddit is not accessible to this tool. The private school business-office conversation appears to happen in closed Facebook groups, association listservs, and diocesan channels I cannot read. So I have institutional and journalistic pain evidence but no unfiltered practitioner voice. That is a real gap in the case.

## 4. Alternatives

**Verified evidence**

- **Open source: essentially nothing.** GitHub repository search on 2026-08-31 returned `total_count` of 0 for `education savings account ESA school reconciliation` and 0 for `school choice voucher tuition reconciliation`. `tuition accounts receivable school` returned exactly one repository, a zero-star Indonesian school finance project. `school management tuition billing` returned three repositories, all zero stars. There is no open-source starting point and no open-source threat.
- **The state portals themselves are the primary alternative.** Step Up refers to "the school's billing report," so some school-side reporting exists inside EMA ([school important message](https://www.stepupforstudents.org/school-important-message/)). Odyssey's TEFA platform is contracted to serve schools directly and the Comptroller can penalize it for slow transaction processing.

**Reported, not independently verified** (vendor marketing and software aggregator listings, weakest tier of evidence here)

- FACTS Management markets managing "all awarded aid in a single system, including scholarships, school choice funds," with awards automatically credited to student accounts. Blackbaud markets tracking scholarships and tuition discounts in one place and accounting for state vouchers and ESAs.
- Aggregator sites put school management software at roughly $2 to $15 per student per year, entry-level small-school total cost at roughly $1,500 to $5,000 per year, and Blackbaud at roughly $20,000 to $28,000 per year. FACTS and Blackbaud do not publish prices, so these are third-party estimates and should not be treated as quotes.
- A Denver startup selling program-administration software to state governments may receive $2 million from Florida to build a platform for the new federally funded scholarships (Colorado Public Education Watch).

**Inference.** The incumbents track *awarded* aid on the school's own ledger. That is a different thing from reconciling *what the intermediary actually paid, per student, per quarter, after carry-forward splits and mid-year clawbacks*. The gap is real today. It is also narrow, and any of FACTS, Blackbaud, or Odyssey could close it as a feature. The spreadsheet is the true incumbent and it is free.

## 5. Wedge

**The narrow entry point:** the parent approval queue.

A school with 150 scholarship students has 150 invoices per quarter that cannot pay until a specific parent logs into a specific portal and clicks approve within thirty days. Nobody owns that queue. The school cannot see it well, the parent forgets, and the money silently does not arrive. This is the smallest possible slice of the problem, it is the one with the clearest causal line to cash, and it is one a solo builder can serve without touching anyone's accounting system: import roster, track invoice state, nudge the parent by SMS and email, show the school a single number for dollars stuck in unapproved invoices.

**Inference on why a solo builder can win this slice.** Step Up will not build it because chasing parents on behalf of schools is not its mandate, and because Florida statute caps a funding organization's administrative spend at 3% of revenue, with the Legislature currently debating a cut to 2% (Verified evidence, [Jacksonville Today](https://jaxtoday.org/2026/02/22/florida-private-schools-sue-step-up-for-students-over-voucher-payout-problems/)). Odyssey is contractually focused on platform uptime and transaction speed, not on a school's collections workflow. FACTS and Blackbaud sell to schools large enough to buy a suite, and the median participating school is small.

**The wedge's fatal dependency, stated plainly.** Everything above assumes the school can get invoice state out of the portal in bulk. If it cannot, the wedge collapses into manual data entry and dies.

## 6. MVP

**Smallest shippable scope**

1. School uploads a roster with per-student contracted tuition and fees, and the family contact for each student.
2. School uploads whatever billing or payment report it can export from the state portal, as CSV or PDF.
3. The system reconciles the two per student, per quarter, and produces four buckets: funded and paid, invoiced and awaiting parent approval, invoiced and paid short, and enrolled with no award visible at all.
4. Parent nudge sequence by email and SMS for the second bucket, with an approval deadline countdown.
5. One export: a dated, source-linked statement of what the school believes it is owed, per student, suitable for handing to the funding organization, a board, or an attorney.

**Explicitly out of scope for v1:** general ledger integration, financial aid awarding, enrollment management, parent portal, anything that touches a payment rail.

**Build effort, labeled as Inference.** Six to ten weeks of solo work for the reconciliation core and the nudge engine, on the assumption that the portal export is a structured file. If the input is a PDF of unpredictable layout, add four to six weeks and accept an accuracy ceiling. If there is no export at all, do not build.

## 7. AI role

**A specific workflow advantage, not a wrapper.** The reconciliation input is heterogeneous by construction: Florida EMA billing reports, Texas Odyssey remittance data, and eventually six or more other Odyssey states, each with its own layout, plus each state's own carry-forward and split-invoice conventions. Document extraction and schema normalization over that mess is a legitimate model task, and the cost of getting it wrong is bounded because a human reviews the reconciliation before it becomes a claim.

**Where AI adds little, stated plainly.** The matching logic itself is deterministic arithmetic against a roster. The nudge sequence is a scheduler. Anything generative in the dispute letter is cosmetic. If this product is described as "AI-powered," that is marketing, not architecture. Roughly one component of five benefits from a model.

## 8. Pricing

**Proposed model, all of it Inference anchored to the alternatives in section 4.**

- Per-school subscription with a band on scholarship-student count, not per-seat. Roughly $99 per month up to 100 scholarship students, $199 up to 300, $349 above that, billed annually through Stripe.
- Rationale for the anchor: the aggregator estimate of $1,500 to $5,000 per year for entry-level school management software means a $1,200 to $4,200 annual line item is inside a small school's existing software mental model rather than a new category of spend.
- Explicitly reject percentage-of-recovery pricing. It would make the vendor look like a collections agency operating against a state program, it invites regulatory attention in exactly the states currently rewriting these rules, and Texas law already restricts how TEFA money can move.

**Assumption.** That a school owed $50,000 will spend $2,000 a year to see the $50,000 more clearly. Untested, and it is the single most important untested assumption in this entry.

## 9. Distribution

**Verified evidence that the buyer list is public and addressable.** Florida publishes a private school directory of schools eligible to serve scholarship students, linked from Step Up's own site. Texas published a map of participating private schools, reported at nearly 800 schools. Named plaintiff schools and the named legislative witness are public.

**First 100 customers, as Inference.**

1. Start with the roughly 800 Texas schools in their first year on TEFA. They have no established process, no institutional workaround, and an immediate concrete question about whether Q1 money arrived. Cold outreach to a first-year cohort converts better than to a five-year-scarred one.
2. Go association-first rather than school-first: state private school associations, diocesan superintendent offices, and classical and Christian school networks. One diocesan endorsement is worth fifty cold emails, and these networks already circulate operational guidance about ESA participation.
3. Publish a free, genuinely useful quarterly reconciliation worksheet for each state's payment calendar. The schools that download it are self-identified buyers.

**Risk to the channel.** Step Up's site states that using third-party services to access accounts violates its user agreement. A vendor perceived as encouraging schools to route around the funding organization could be blocked from association channels. The product has to be positioned as the school's own books, never as portal automation.

## 10. Risks

1. **No machine-readable export exists.** The product becomes manual entry and no small school office will sustain it. This is the top risk and it is unresolved.
2. **The terms-of-service wall.** Step Up states plainly on its site that third-party account access violates its user agreement. Any scraping or credentialed automation path is closed, and a vendor that tries it invites being named publicly by a $1 billion counterparty.
3. **The state fixes the problem.** Florida SB 318, the cross-check systems in progress, the FEFP funding-formula debate, and the proposal to tie payments to student identifier numbers all point at the exact failure being monetized. If Florida succeeds, the Florida half of the market thins out.
4. **Odyssey ships a school dashboard.** It is contracted to serve schools, it is penalized for transaction friction, and it operates in eight states. If it builds this, the Texas half of the market disappears without warning.
5. **Visibility is not a purchase.** The documented responses to this pain so far have been legislative testimony and a lawsuit, not software procurement. There is no evidence in anything I read that a school has ever paid a vendor to solve this.
6. **Buyer poverty correlates with buyer pain.** The schools with the worst cash gaps are the least able to add a subscription, and some of them are the ones closing.
7. **Political and reputational volatility.** Building a business whose revenue depends on the operational condition of a contested school-choice program means every legislative session is a product risk.

## 11. Kill criteria

Falsifiable, with thresholds, all runnable in about two weeks of phone and email work and zero code.

- **Export test.** Ask ten participating schools, five in Florida and five in Texas, to describe or show what they can download about per-student payments. **Kill if fewer than six of ten can produce a structured file rather than a screen they read manually.**
- **Willingness-to-pay test.** In the same ten conversations, present the four-bucket reconciliation view as a mock and ask for a signed letter of intent at $1,200 per year. **Kill if fewer than three of ten will sign.**
- **Unapproved-invoice test.** Ask each school what dollar amount is currently sitting in invoices a parent has not approved. **Kill if the median answer is under $5,000, because the wedge is then too small to fund a subscription.**
- **Incumbent test.** Ask the same schools whether FACTS, Blackbaud, or their SIS already shows them this. **Kill if more than four of ten say yes.**
- **Channel test.** Contact three state private school associations and two diocesan offices. **Kill if none will circulate a neutral reconciliation worksheet to members within four weeks.**
- **Regulatory test.** Read Step Up's and Odyssey's school and provider user agreements in full. **Kill if either prohibits a school from sharing its own payment data with a third-party processor.**

## 12. Verdict: Watch

The demand side clears the bar and then some. The pain is on the legislative record in a named school head's testimony, in a state audit, in a $47 million shortfall, and in a lawsuit by seven schools. The mechanism is documented by the counterparty itself, including a page that opens by conceding schools are confused. The trend is structural across two mega-states and eight Odyssey states, with a federal scholarship arriving in 2027. The competitive floor is unusually empty: GitHub returns literally zero repositories for the core queries.

It is not a Build, for four reasons that all sit on the supply side.

The first is that I could not verify the one thing the product cannot exist without, which is a bulk export of per-student payment state from either portal. The second is that the most valuable form of automation is explicitly foreclosed by Step Up's stated user agreement, which I read on their own site. The third is that this is a flashlight and not a fix, and I found no instance of any school paying money for the flashlight, only instances of schools hiring lawyers and testifying to legislators. The fourth is that the gap is being actively closed from two directions at once, by a Florida legislature that has already passed a reform bill through the Senate and by a well-funded multi-state vendor that is contractually motivated to reduce exactly this friction.

Watch, with a specific trigger for revisiting: if the two-week validation in section 11 clears the export test and the willingness-to-pay test, this moves to Build immediately, because everything else about it is unusually strong. Until then it stays in the journal and is not promoted to the idea grill.
