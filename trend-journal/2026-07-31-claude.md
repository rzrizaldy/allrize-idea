# California DROP 45-Day Cycle Runner for Small Registered Data Brokers — 2026-07-31

**Contributor:** Claude (Cowork scheduled task)
**File:** `trend-journal/2026-07-31-claude.md`
**Market:** United States first (California-scoped statute); Indonesia relevance: not established
**Motion:** B2B. The registered data broker's compliance or engineering owner pays; a privacy operations person and a data engineer use it.
**Verdict:** Watch

## Summary

California's Delete Act obliges every registered data broker to pull hashed consumer deletion lists from the state's Delete Request and Opt-out Platform (DROP) at least once every 45 days, standardize and hash its own records to a precisely published spec, match, delete or exempt, and upload a status code for every work item. The obligation starts tomorrow, 2026-08-01, and the penalty is $200 per deletion request per day. The workflow is unusually well suited to a solo builder: the state publishes an exact, deterministic specification with test vectors, the buyer list is public and finite, and the cycle repeats forever. It is a Watch rather than a Build for three reasons that the evidence below supports: the buyer population is capped at roughly 575 to 600 registered brokers, at least three vendors already sell this exact module including one enterprise platform that shipped a purpose-built DROP product in June 2026, and the deadline-driven scramble that would have been the distribution wedge ends the day this entry is written.

## Verified evidence

- **The obligation, the cadence, and the exact date, collected 2026-07-31.** CalPrivacy states that beginning **August 1, 2026**, data brokers must access the accessible deletion mechanism at least once every 45 days and process consumer deletion requests, citing Cal. Civ. Code 1798.99.86(c). Registration is annual between January 1 and 31, and the 2026 fee is $6,000 plus a payment processing fee. [CPPA information for data brokers](https://cppa.ca.gov/data_brokers/) · [privacy.ca.gov DROP for data brokers](https://privacy.ca.gov/drop-for-data-brokers/)
- **Penalty structure, collected 2026-07-31.** CalPrivacy publishes two penalties: $200 per day for failure to register, and **$200 per day per deletion request** for each day a broker fails to delete information as required, plus the agency's cost of investigation and administrative action. [privacy.ca.gov penalties section](https://privacy.ca.gov/drop-for-data-brokers/)
- **Independent legal confirmation and enforcement posture, collected 2026-07-31.** Fenwick's 2026-06-18 client alert states that over 300,000 California residents had registered and submitted deletion requests through DROP, that fines apply whether or not the broker is registered, and that CalPrivacy has formed a Data Broker Enforcement Strike Force and already brought public enforcement actions, including a $56,600 fine against ROR Partners LLC in December 2025 for failing to register. The alert also confirms the downstream obligation to service providers and contractors, the requirement to maintain suppression lists so deleted data stays deleted, and independent third-party audits every three years beginning January 1, 2028 with six-year retention. [Fenwick alert](https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/dont-drop-ball-upcoming-changes-california-delete-act-five-key-steps-companies)
- **Buyer population size, collected 2026-07-31.** The National Law Review, reporting on a CPPA meeting, states that more than **575 data brokers** have registered with DROP, over 242,000 California residents signed up, and more than 18,000 deletion requests were submitted within 48 hours of the January 1, 2026 launch. [National Law Review, 2026-03-05](https://natlawreview.com/article/compliance-wave-coming-data-brokers-brace-drop-deletion-requests-under-delete-act) A July 2026 trade article puts the figure at "600-plus data brokers" and roughly 300,000 queued requests. Trade press is weaker than the agency's own count, so the defensible range is roughly 575 to 600. [TechTimes, 2026-07-29](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/321938/20260729/california-drop-mandate-starts-saturday-200-per-day-fines-hit-600-plus-data-brokers.htm)
- **The technical spec is fully published and deterministic, collected 2026-07-31.** CalPrivacy publishes six consumer deletion list types (`NDZ`, `Email`, `Phone`, `MAID`, `NameVIN`, `CTVID`), a `GET`-style ZIP download of one CSV per selected list, a fixed file naming convention `<YYYYMMDD>_<DataBrokerId>_<DataType>[_<Suffix>].csv`, an `ID,Hash` download schema, an `Id,Status` upload schema, four status codes (2 Exempted, 3 Deleted, 4 Opted out, 5 Not found), per-field standardization rules, SHA-256 with UTF-8 input and Base64 output, composite hashing for `NDZ` and `NameVIN`, and worked test vectors. A DROP Sandbox with a standardization and hashing tool has been available since March 2026. [privacy.ca.gov, Working with the data](https://privacy.ca.gov/drop-for-data-brokers/technical-specifications/working-with-data/)
- **Enterprise incumbent already shipped a purpose-built DROP module, collected 2026-07-31.** DataGrail's DROP solution page, published 2026-06-22, markets fully automated list download, hash-to-hash matching, deletion execution, status upload, suppression list hosting, automatic application of the §7613 first-party exemption, single-tenant architecture with per-customer AWS keys, and 2,500+ integrations. Its own FAQ concedes an in-house build is possible and states that DataGrail "provides open-source libraries and a mock DROP server for teams that want to pre-hash." No price is published. This is vendor-authored marketing, so it evidences feature scope and market entry, not customer outcomes. [DataGrail DROP solution](https://www.datagrail.io/solutions/drop-compliance/)

Ketch and OneTrust have both published DROP compliance content. ## Inference

- Reasoned from the published specification: this is one of the rare compliance products where the hard part is not judgment but exactness. Standardization rules, hash construction, file naming, and status codes are all fixed and testable, and CalPrivacy ships a sandbox with a verification tool. A single competent developer can reach a correct implementation with high confidence, which is unusual for a compliance category.
- Reasoned from the penalty structure and the 45-day cadence: the failure mode brokers actually fear is a missed cycle, not a wrong architecture. That points at a narrow product shaped like a cron plus an audit trail rather than a privacy platform.
- Reasoned from the DataGrail page combined with the specialist vendors: the enterprise end of the market is already served, and the specialist end is already claimed by at least two named products, one of which is built on a low-code app builder judging by the Lovable preview image referenced in its own social card metadata. That is the signature of another solo or very small builder occupying the same wedge.
- Reasoned from the registry count of roughly 575 to 600 and the fact that registration costs $6,000 a year: every buyer in this market has already demonstrated willingness to pay a four-figure annual sum for the privilege of operating, which is a favorable qualification signal. But the total buyer count is small enough that this is a services-scale market, not a venture-scale one. At $300 per month, capturing 10 percent of the registry produces roughly $200,000 in annual recurring revenue, which is a good solo outcome but leaves no room for paid acquisition.
- Reasoned from the date: the compliance scramble that would have driven a cold-outreach campaign ends on 2026-08-01. A builder starting today arrives after the buying decision for most prepared brokers. The remaining demand is the unprepared tail plus post-enforcement replacements, which is a slower and less predictable funnel.

## Assumptions

- That a meaningful share of the registry consists of small brokers with data in one or two databases who have not already bought an enterprise privacy platform. The registry is public but was not analyzed by company size in this session.
- That those brokers would buy tooling rather than have an in-house engineer write a few hundred lines against a fully documented spec. Given how clean the spec is, in-house build is a serious substitute and this is the assumption most likely to be wrong.
- That selling software into a category under active enforcement does not create unacceptable liability exposure for a solo vendor, particularly around claims of correctness in matching and deletion. This must be reviewed with qualified US counsel before any compliance claim is made in marketing.
- That prospects would accept an architecture in which the vendor never touches raw personal information. Any design that ingests broker PII would raise the security bar beyond what a solo operator should accept.
- That no additional state replicates DROP soon enough to expand the buyer population within the payback window.

## Consulting assessment

### 1. Trend durability

This is a statutory obligation, not a fashion. SB 362 fixes an indefinite 45-day cycle, CalPrivacy has built and shipped the platform, over 242,000 residents have signed up, and the regime deepens over time: third-party audits begin January 1, 2028 on a three-year cycle with six-year record retention, and SB 361 expanded annual disclosure requirements in 2025. The durability risk is legislative or judicial repeal, not fading interest. **Verified** from the CPPA and Fenwick sources above. No Google Trends claim is made, and none is needed.

### 2. Customer and JTBD

**Motion:** B2B. **Who pays:** the compliance, legal operations, or engineering owner at a registered data broker. **Who uses:** a privacy operations person who selects lists and reviews cycle results, plus a data engineer who connects the record source once. **Job to be done:** "Make sure I can prove, every 45 days and forever, that I pulled the current DROP lists, matched them correctly, acted on every work item, and uploaded a status for each one, without ever handing my raw customer data to a third party." The job is evidence and calendar discipline, not data science.

### 3. Pain evidence

The pain here is documented in regulatory and legal-analysis sources rather than forum complaints, and this entry did not find first-person buyer quotes. That is a real gap. What is verified: $200 per request per day with no cap (CalPrivacy); Fenwick's worked example that 50,000 unprocessed requests could produce $10 million of exposure per day; an existing $56,600 fine against a company that did not think it was a data broker; a dedicated CalPrivacy enforcement strike force; and DataGrail's own framing that "building and maintaining a DROP process is a major engineering project data brokers can't afford to take on." The last is marketing and should be discounted. **No independent buyer complaint thread was located, and none is claimed.** Finding three real brokers who will describe their current process in their own words is the first validation step this entry did not complete.

### 4. Alternatives

- **Do it in-house.** Free, fully specified, sandbox provided. This is the strongest competitor.
- **Enterprise privacy platforms.** DataGrail shipped a dedicated DROP module in June 2026. OneTrust and Ketch have published DROP guidance. Pricing is not public for any of them; enterprise privacy platforms are commonly five figures and up annually, but that is **inference, not a verified price**.
- **Data broker compliance specialists.** Superset covers DROP alongside state registrations and privacy inbox management. BrokerDeleteOps markets the 45-day cycle explicitly. Neither publishes pricing on the pages reviewed.
- **Law firms and consultants.** Fenwick, Kelley Drye, Troutman, Freshfields and others are publishing readiness guidance, which converts into advisory engagements rather than software.
- **Spreadsheets plus a script.** Viable for a broker with a single identifier type and one database.

### 5. Wedge

The narrow entry point is **the unattended cycle runner with an audit binder, for brokers with one or two identifier types and a single record store.** Concretely: connect one Postgres, Snowflake, BigQuery, or S3 source; select your lists; the product runs the download, hashing, matching, status upload, and suppression list append on schedule; and it produces a dated, immutable evidence pack per cycle. Two things differentiate it from the enterprise platforms: raw personal information never leaves the customer's boundary, because the hashing runs in a customer-side agent or query and only Base64 hashes and work item IDs cross the wire; and the price is a fraction of a platform seat because the product does exactly one regulation. The enterprise vendors cannot easily go down-market without cannibalizing platform deals, and the in-house build competes on day one but loses on day 400, when the person who wrote the script has left and nobody can produce the 2028 audit evidence.

### 6. MVP

Smallest shippable scope, in order:
1. DROP API credential storage and list selection for the six list types.
2. Scheduled ZIP download, per-list CSV parsing, and removed-identifier handling.
3. A customer-side hashing agent implementing the published standardization rules and composite hashing, validated against the CalPrivacy sandbox test vectors, run as a container or a generated SQL query so raw values never leave the customer.
4. Hash-to-hash match, status assignment across codes 2, 3, 4, 5, upload file generation with the exact required filename, and upload.
5. Append-only suppression list of matched hashes, with a re-check hook for newly acquired data.
6. Per-cycle evidence pack: what was downloaded, counts by status, timestamps, spec version, and an operator attestation, exported as PDF and CSV.
7. Missed-cycle alerting at day 30 and day 40.
Stripe for billing, role-based access, and an audit log round it out. **Inference on effort:** six to ten focused weeks for a strong developer, with most of the risk in item 3 rather than anywhere else. Deletion execution against customer systems is deliberately excluded from the MVP; the product instructs and records, the customer's own system deletes.

### 7. AI role

**AI adds little to the core of this product and should be kept out of it.** Matching is exact hash equality. Standardization is a specified string transform. Status codes are a small decision table. Introducing a language model anywhere in that path adds nondeterminism to a workflow whose entire value is being provably correct. The two legitimate and narrow uses are outside the compliance path: drafting the plain-English cycle summary that goes in front of an auditor, and helping a prospect map their column names to the six identifier types during onboarding, with a human confirming. Any marketing that leads with AI here would be actively counterproductive with this buyer.

### 8. Pricing

Proposed: a single self-serve tier at **$249 per month** or **$2,490 per year**, covering one broker entity, all six lists, one record source, unlimited cycles, and evidence packs. A **$599 per month** tier adds multiple record sources, service provider routing records, and a named contact. Anchors, in order of confidence: the state's own $6,000 annual registration fee is verified and sets the buyer's baseline expectation for what Delete Act compliance costs per year, so a product priced under half the state fee is easy to approve. Enterprise platform pricing is not published and is **not claimed** here. A one-time **$1,500 implementation and sandbox validation** fee is defensible and should be offered, because the first correct cycle is the thing the buyer actually wants.

### 9. Distribution

The first 100 customers come from the fact that **the buyer list is public**. The CPPA maintains a data broker registry naming every registered broker, and SB 361 expanded the disclosures each one files. The concrete sequence: pull the registry, enrich for company size and engineering headcount, and segment out the roughly 400 to 500 names that are too small to be enterprise platform customers. Then a direct sequence built on a free, useful artifact rather than a pitch: an open-source, MIT-licensed reference implementation of the CalPrivacy standardization and hashing spec with the published test vectors as a test suite, published on GitHub and posted where privacy engineers read. The paid product is the scheduler, the suppression list, and the evidence binder around that free core. Secondary channels: the IAPP community, privacy counsel at the firms already publishing DROP alerts who need a tool to point small clients at, and CalPrivacy's own data broker mailing list as a monitoring input rather than a channel. **Assumption:** cold outbound to a named, regulated, deadline-bound list converts better than typical B2B cold outbound. Not verified.

### 10. Risks

- **Market size ceiling.** Roughly 575 to 600 total buyers, shrinking at the top where the enterprise vendors already sell. This caps the business at solo scale permanently.
- **Timing.** The deadline is tomorrow. The urgency-driven land grab is over as this is written.
- **In-house substitution.** The spec is so clean that a competent engineer can implement it in a sprint. The product must sell durability and evidence, not difficulty, and that is a harder sale.
- **Occupied wedge.** BrokerDeleteOps and Superset already describe this exact scope. A third entrant needs a reason to exist beyond price.
- **Liability and trust.** Selling correctness into a $200-per-request-per-day penalty regime invites contractual exposure. Even a hash-only architecture requires a security posture, and small brokers may still demand SOC 2, which is a real cost for a solo operator.
- **Spec drift.** CalPrivacy can revise the standardization rules, add list types, or change status semantics. That is maintenance the customer is paying to outsource, but it is also unbounded work.
- **Category reputation.** The customer is the data broker industry. That is a legitimate business, but it constrains hiring, partnerships, and public storytelling, and it should be a deliberate choice rather than an accident.

### 11. Kill criteria

Falsifiable, with thresholds:
- **Discovery.** Contact 40 brokers from the public registry within 30 days. If fewer than 8 agree to a call, the list-based channel does not work and the plan's only distribution advantage is gone. **Kill.**
- **In-house substitution.** If, among the first 10 discovery calls, 7 or more say they already built it in-house or have it covered by an existing platform, the addressable share of the registry is too thin. **Kill.**
- **Willingness to pay.** If fewer than 3 of the first 20 qualified prospects will commit to $249 per month or a $1,500 paid implementation before the product is finished, price and value are not aligned. **Kill.**
- **Paid conversion.** If 90 days after launch there are fewer than 10 paying accounts, the ceiling math no longer supports continued full-time work. **Downgrade to maintenance.**
- **Competitive.** If DataGrail, Ketch, or OneTrust publishes a self-serve DROP tier under $500 per month, the wedge closes. **Kill.**
- **Regulatory.** If the August 1 obligation is enjoined, stayed, or materially delayed, the demand driver disappears. **Kill immediately.**

### 12. Verdict: Watch

Not Build, and not Reject. The reasoning that drove it, in order of weight:

The evidence quality here is the best in this journal so far. The obligation is statutory and dated, the penalty is published by the regulator itself, the technical spec is complete with test vectors and a sandbox, and the buyer list is a public government registry. That combination normally argues for Build.

Three facts outweigh it. First, the buyer population is hard-capped at roughly 575 to 600 and the top of it is already served by an enterprise vendor that shipped a purpose-built module six weeks ago. Second, at least two specialists already market the identical scope, one of them apparently built by a small team on a low-code stack, which is exactly the competitor profile that beat the CAM reconciliation idea in the 2026-07-30 Claude entry. Third, and decisively for timing, the compliance deadline that would have been the distribution wedge expires the day after this entry is written. Building a deadline product after the deadline means selling to the tail rather than the wave.

What would change this to Build: verified first-person evidence from three or more small registered brokers that they are entering the August 1 cycle without tooling and without an in-house implementation, plus two of them committing to a paid implementation. That is a two-week validation sprint against a public list, and it is cheap. Until that exists, this is a Watch.
