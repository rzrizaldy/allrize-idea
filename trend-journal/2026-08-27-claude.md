# DROP Cycle Runner: California Delete Act deletion-cycle tooling for small registered data brokers — 2026-08-27

**Contributor:** Claude (Cowork scheduled task)
**File:** `trend-journal/2026-08-27-claude.md`
**Market:** United States first (California only at launch; Connecticut from late 2028). Indonesia relevance: not established.
**Motion:** B2B. The registered data broker pays and uses.
**Verdict:** **Watch**

## Summary

California's Delete Request and Opt-out Platform (DROP) became operative for data brokers on 1 August 2026. Every registered data broker must now download hashed consumer deletion lists from a state API at least once every 45 calendar days, standardize and hash their own records, match, delete, cascade the deletion to service providers, maintain a permanent suppression list, and report a status code for every request back into DROP. This repeats forever. The penalty is USD 200 per request per day.

Three weeks after the deadline, the regulator reported that only about 30 percent of registered brokers had begun processing. That is a large, current, quantified compliance gap in a market whose entire buyer list is published by the state with contact emails attached, and whose technical specification, OpenAPI schema, and sandbox are fully public. One developer can build the cycle runner.

The verdict is Watch rather than Build for reasons that sit on the supply side, not the demand side. The wedge is squeezed from below by a legally sufficient free manual path that the regulator itself provides, and from above by funded incumbents (DataGrail shipped a purpose-built DROP module before the deadline; Ketch, Transcend, and TrustArc all market to the same list). The total addressable buyer count is roughly 500 to 600 entities. And the buyer is, by definition, a company under active regulator scrutiny for its data handling, which makes an unaudited solo vendor a hard sell. Every one of those doubts is cheap to test with two weeks of outreach against a public email list, before any code is written.

## 1. Trend durability

This is a scheduled regulatory ratchet, not a spike. The evidence is a sequence of dated, already-enacted obligations that escalate through 2028.

### Verified evidence

- The Delete Act (SB 362, signed October 2023) directed the creation of DROP. Consumers could submit requests from 1 January 2026. Data brokers "must access DROP and process these deletion requests beginning August 1, 2026," repeating "at least once every 45 calendar days." Source: [privacy.ca.gov, DROP for data brokers](https://privacy.ca.gov/drop-for-data-brokers/) and [Processing DROP requests](https://privacy.ca.gov/drop-for-data-brokers/process-drop-requests/).
- At the CalPrivacy board meeting of 7 August 2026, the agency initiated formal rulemaking for independent third-party DROP compliance audits, in preparation for the Delete Act audit requirements that take effect 1 January 2028. Audits would be triennial and would examine deletion logs and status reports. The draft entered a 45-day public comment period. Source: [IAPP, 10 Aug. 2026](https://iapp.org/news/a/calprivacy-discusses-drop-enforcement-data-broker-fee-hike).
- The same board approved raising the 2027 data broker registration and access fee to USD 9,500, an increase of USD 3,500 over the 2026 fee. Source: [IAPP, 10 Aug. 2026](https://iapp.org/news/a/calprivacy-discusses-drop-enforcement-data-broker-fee-hike).
- Connecticut SB 4 was signed 27 May 2026 (Public Act 26-64). It creates a data broker registry with registration beginning 1 January 2027, requires the Commissioner to establish an accessible deletion mechanism by 1 July 2028, and from 1 October 2028 requires registered brokers to check that mechanism at least once every 45 days. Sources: [Fox Rothschild](https://dataprivacy.foxrothschild.com/2026/06/articles/general-privacy-data-security-news-developments/connecticuts-new-data-broker-law-sb-4-what-businesses-need-to-know/), [PrivacyLawMap](https://privacylawmap.com/blog/connecticut-ctdpa-amendments-sb4-2026).
- Vermont amended its data broker law on 16 June 2026, adding annual registration obligations effective 1 January 2027 and directing the Secretary of State to develop a universal deletion mechanism. Source: search summary of [PrivacyLawMap state-by-state guide](https://privacylawmap.com/blog/data-broker-registration-requirements-2026). I did not fetch the underlying Vermont bill text.

### Inference

- Reasoning from the Connecticut 45-day cadence being identical to California's, and from Vermont's directive: the operational shape of the job (periodic list retrieval, hash matching, status reporting, suppression list maintenance) is being copied between states. A tool built once for California has a credible path to becoming a multi-state clearinghouse client by 2028 rather than a single-jurisdiction utility. This is the strongest durability argument available.
- Reasoning from the fee increase plus the audit rulemaking landing in the same board meeting: CalPrivacy is funding and formalizing sustained oversight rather than treating 1 August as a one-time milestone.

### Assumptions

- That Connecticut's 2028 mechanism will be technically similar enough to California's that shared tooling is viable. Unverified. Connecticut has not published a specification.
- That no federal preemption arrives before 2028 to flatten the state-by-state regime.

## 2. Customer and JTBD

**Motion:** B2B. **Who pays:** the registered data broker (the legal entity on the CalPrivacy registry). **Who uses:** in a small broker, the same one or two people who did the registration, typically a compliance-minded operations lead or the founder, not a privacy engineer.

**Segment I would target:** the small "pass-through" list broker. This is a specific, documented category, not a guess. See §3.

**Job to be done:** "Every 45 days, without fail, and forever, prove to California that I processed every deletion request, without hiring an engineer and without handing my customer data to a stranger."

The job has three sub-jobs the buyer actually feels:

1. **Never miss a cycle.** The clock is unforgiving and the penalty compounds daily per request.
2. **Get the hashing exactly right.** Match rate is a compliance artifact. Wrong standardization means false "NOT FOUND" statuses reported to a regulator that will audit deletion logs from 2028.
3. **Produce the evidence.** Status codes reported into DROP, plus a suppression list maintained in perpetuity against future acquisitions.

### Verified evidence

- The four required status codes are Deleted, Opted out, Exempted, and Not found. Status must be reported within 45 days of downloading the request. Source: [Processing DROP requests](https://privacy.ca.gov/drop-for-data-brokers/process-drop-requests/).
- Six deletion lists exist: name plus date of birth plus ZIP; email; phone; mobile advertising ID; name plus VIN; connected TV identifier. A broker must select all lists whose identifiers could match its records. Source: same page, citing Cal. Code Regs. tit. 11, § 7610(a)(3).
- Standardization is prescriptive: lowercase, remove special characters, specific formats for dates, phone numbers, and ZIP codes, and a defined multi-identifier concatenation protocol. Source: same page.
- Unmatched requests still create a permanent obligation. The broker must maintain a suppression list and screen newly collected records against it before selling or sharing, and must update the status to Deleted if a match appears later. Source: same page, "Ongoing requirements."

### Inference

- Reasoning from the four status codes plus the permanent suppression obligation: the deliverable the buyer is really purchasing is an audit trail, not a deletion. The deletion is a side effect. That reframing matters for how the product is positioned and priced.

### Assumptions

- That the small-broker buyer is a non-engineer. Consistent with the pass-through characterization in §3 but not directly verified per company.

## 3. Pain evidence

This is the strongest section of the entry. The pain is documented on the record by a named practitioner, quantified by the regulator, and corroborated by three competing vendors who have no incentive to agree with each other about anything except that the job is hard.

### Verified evidence

**Regulator-quantified non-compliance.** At the 7 August 2026 board meeting, CalPrivacy "noted 30% of registered data brokers began processing DROP requests in the first week following the compliance deadline, with 450,000 requests submitted to date." Source: [IAPP, 10 Aug. 2026](https://iapp.org/news/a/calprivacy-discusses-drop-enforcement-data-broker-fee-hike).

**Named practitioner testimony on small-broker burden.** Benjamin Isaacson of In-House Privacy, a law firm serving advertising and marketing clients, wrote to CalPrivacy during the DROP rulemaking:

> "Many data brokers are small businesses and the current rulemaking imposes significant technical and operational burdens... many of the registered data brokers do not 'compile' data themselves, but rather 'pass through' data from other data brokers to their clients... many of the burdens being placed on these entities to access and combine data from the DROP to each of their 'passed through lists' every 45 days where they do not maintain any centralized 'database' in which to apply the DROP deletions will lead to their creation of a very large suppression file of every DROP registered user that they will store in perpetuity."

Source: [In-House Privacy comments to the CPPA](https://www.inhouseprivacy.com/blog/in-house-privacys-comments-to-the-cppa-re-sb-362-delete-act-rulemaking-on-the-subject-of-the-accessible-deletion-mechanism).

The same letter states the regulations assume brokers can perform "ID Graphing" or "ID Resolution," and asks that "the technical requirements for applying the DROP should be designated for the lowest common denominator of data brokers, and not the most sophisticated larger businesses." It also asks that the status reporting requirement be eliminated or delayed because it "creates a significant and material cost burden."

**The same practitioner escalated in public comment after the deadline.** At the 7 August board meeting, Isaacson "highlighted the potential for 'significant strain' and burdens among data brokers stemming from an increased fee," and urged the agency to fund DROP from enforcement actions against unregistered brokers instead. Source: [IAPP, 10 Aug. 2026](https://iapp.org/news/a/calprivacy-discusses-drop-enforcement-data-broker-fee-hike).

**Competing vendors independently describe the same burden.**

- DataGrail: "Building and maintaining a DROP process is a major engineering project data brokers can't afford to take on," and building in-house requires "DROP API integration, California's exact hashing specification, matching logic, status reporting, and ongoing suppression list maintenance." Source: [DataGrail DROP solution page](https://www.datagrail.io/solutions/drop-compliance/).
- Ketch: "If your team is handling DSRs through spreadsheets, email threads, and manual stakeholder coordination right now, DROP will break that model." Source: [Ketch blog, updated 25 June 2026](https://www.ketch.com/blog/posts/california-drop-platform-data-broker-compliance).

**The existing request backlog is real and growing.** Three sourced datapoints, from three publishers, with slightly different definitions:

| Date | Figure | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Early 2026" | 215,000+ consumers registered on DROP | [Ketch](https://www.ketch.com/blog/posts/california-drop-platform-data-broker-compliance) |
| Page published June 2026 | 242,000 registered users | [DataGrail](https://www.datagrail.io/solutions/drop-compliance/) |
| 7 August 2026 | 450,000 requests submitted to date | [IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/calprivacy-discusses-drop-enforcement-data-broker-fee-hike) |

**Caveat on that table:** "registered consumers" and "requests submitted" are not the same unit, and the three sources do not define their terms identically. The direction of travel is well supported. The precise growth rate is not.

### Inference

- Reasoning from the 30 percent figure against a registry of roughly 500 to 600 entities: on the order of 350 to 420 registered brokers were not processing in the first week. That is the size of the unserved pool. It is unusually rare to have a regulator publish the exact size of your prospect list.
- Reasoning from Isaacson's two interventions fourteen months apart: the burden he described in rulemaking was not addressed by the final regulations, and he was still raising it after the deadline. The pain persisted through the regulatory process rather than being cured by it.

### Assumptions

- **The critical unverified assumption of this entire entry:** that the 70 percent who had not started represent unserved demand rather than brokers who will never pay for software. The IAPP article notes only the figure, not the reason. This assumption is what holds the verdict at Watch. See §11.
- That "first week" non-processing indicates difficulty rather than brokers deliberately using the full 45-day window, which the regulations permit. The cycle clock starts on download, and a broker is compliant if it downloads within 45 days. A broker that downloads on day 40 is not late.

## 4. Alternatives

### Verified evidence

**The free manual path, provided by the regulator.** CalPrivacy explicitly supports a manual integration. "You will do this either by API (automated) or manually within DROP." A free sandbox with a standardization and hashing tool is available inside every broker's DROP account. Source: [Processing DROP requests](https://privacy.ca.gov/drop-for-data-brokers/process-drop-requests/), [Technical specifications](https://privacy.ca.gov/drop-for-data-brokers/technical-specifications/). This is the floor competitor and it costs zero dollars.

**Build in-house.** The full API specification is published as a downloadable OpenAPI YAML, version 1.2.0, last updated July 2026, with documented endpoints, error handling, rate limits, list schemas, standardization rules, and hashing examples. Source: [Technical specifications](https://privacy.ca.gov/drop-for-data-brokers/technical-specifications/). The spec being this good cuts both ways: it makes the product buildable by a solo founder, and it makes it buildable by the customer's own contractor.

**Purpose-built incumbent product, already shipped.** DataGrail markets "Data Broker Compliance" as "the first end-to-end DROP automation system built for privacy teams," covering the full 45-day cycle, hash-to-hash matching, deletion execution across 2,500+ integrations, automatic application of the § 7613 first-party exemption, hosted suppression list, and status reporting. Single-tenant, per-customer AWS keys, no PII stored. Pricing is demo-gated with no public figure. Source: [DataGrail DROP solution page](https://www.datagrail.io/solutions/drop-compliance/).

**General privacy platforms positioning into DROP.** Ketch publishes pricing: Free at USD 0, Starter at USD 150/month, Plus from USD 499/month billed annually, Pro custom. Critically, **DSR automation is not included in Free, Starter, or Plus.** It is an add-on at Plus and included only at Pro (custom pricing, positioned for "mid-market-to-enterprise"). Source: [Ketch pricing](https://www.ketch.com/pricing). Transcend, TrustArc, Osano, OneTrust, and Captain Compliance are all publishing DROP content and selling into the same list.

**Vendor claim on integration cost.** Ketch states that "integrations that cost USD 20,000 to USD 50,000 per system with other vendors are included in Ketch." Source: [Ketch blog](https://www.ketch.com/blog/posts/california-drop-platform-data-broker-compliance). This is a competitor's marketing claim about rivals and I have not independently verified it.

**Open source.** DataGrail's own FAQ states it "provides open-source libraries and a mock DROP server for teams that want to pre-hash." I browsed [github.com/datagrail](https://github.com/datagrail) and its 18 public repositories are consent SDKs, Terraform modules, and forks. I did not find a public DROP hashing library or mock server in that organization. Either it is not yet published, is in a package registry rather than a repository, or is distributed to customers. **Unresolved.** I found no active independent open source DROP client.

### Inference

- Reasoning from Ketch's tier structure: the DROP-relevant capability sits at their Pro tier or as a Plus add-on, both of which are quote-based. The published USD 150 and USD 499 tiers are consent management and do not cover DROP. So there is no self-serve, publicly priced DROP product on the market that I could find. That is a real gap.
- Reasoning from DataGrail's positioning ("2,500+ integrations," "no limits, 10 requests or 10,000," named onboarding partner, single-tenant architecture): they are built for the broker with data in ten systems. They are structurally uninterested in the broker with one CSV.

### Assumptions

- That DataGrail, Ketch, and Transcend will not launch a low-priced self-serve DROP tier. Given that DROP is a fixed-size market and their sales motion is enterprise, a downmarket move is possible but not their pattern.

## 5. Wedge

**The narrow entry point: the pass-through broker who has one list, not ten systems, and who will not send personal information to anybody's cloud.**

The wedge product is not a privacy platform. It is a scheduler plus a hasher plus a ledger:

1. A **local-only hashing utility** (CLI and a single-binary desktop app) that reads the broker's CSV or database export, applies CalPrivacy's exact standardization and hashing rules, and emits only hashes. Raw personal information never leaves the customer's machine. This is the trust wedge and it is a stronger security posture than the incumbents, who ingest hashed identifiers into their own vaults.
2. A **hosted cycle runner** that holds only DROP credentials and hashed values, pulls the deletion lists on schedule, performs hash-to-hash matching, and reports status codes back through the API.
3. A **suppression ledger** that persists across cycles, screens new hashed batches before they are sold or shared, and detects the "was NOT FOUND, is now a match" case that triggers a required status amendment.
4. An **audit binder** that exports every cycle as dated, signed evidence in the shape the 2028 triennial audit will ask for.

### Verified evidence that this wedge is legally available

CalPrivacy's Final Statement of Reasons addressed commenters who asked that brokers be allowed to delegate DROP account management to service providers. The agency's response was that § 7610(a)(1) "already addresses the use of a data broker's credentials to access the DROP," limiting it to persons authorized to act on the broker's behalf, with the data broker remaining responsible for all actions taken through its account. Source: search summary of [CPPA DROP FSOR](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/pdf/drop_fsor_45day.pdf) and the [DROP regulations](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/pdf/data_broker_drop_reg.pdf). **I did not fetch the full PDF text; this reading rests on a search summary and should be confirmed against the primary document before any build decision.**

### Inference

- Reasoning from the incumbents' own architecture claims: DataGrail's differentiation is breadth of integrations. Against a broker with a single flat file, breadth is worth nothing, and the customer is paying for capability they cannot use. Narrowness is the advantage here, not a limitation.
- Reasoning from the local-hashing design: it converts the solo builder's biggest liability (why would a privacy-scrutinized company trust an unaudited vendor with PII?) into the product's headline feature (this vendor never receives your PII, by construction). It does not eliminate the SOC 2 conversation, but it changes what is in scope for it.

### Assumptions

- That a meaningful number of registered brokers really are single-list operations. Isaacson describes the category credibly, but I have no count.

## 6. MVP

**Smallest shippable scope, in order:**

1. Open source the hashing and standardization library first, free, matching CalPrivacy's published rules, with the agency's own sandbox tool as the test oracle. This is the distribution artifact, not the product. (Verified: the sandbox includes a standardization and hashing tool for verifying an implementation against fake data. Source: [Processing DROP requests](https://privacy.ca.gov/drop-for-data-brokers/process-drop-requests/).)
2. Paid hosted cycle runner: DROP credential storage, scheduled list download against the published API, hash matching, status upload, cycle-deadline alerting.
3. Suppression ledger with new-batch screening and status amendment detection.
4. Audit binder export.

**Build effort estimate (Inference, not verified):** roughly 6 to 10 weeks for one experienced developer to reach step 2, given that the API specification, hashing rules, status codes, and a sandbox are all published and free. Steps 3 and 4 add perhaps 4 more weeks. The dominant cost is not code. It is the security and assurance posture: SOC 2 Type II, a penetration test, and a defensible data processing agreement, which is months of elapsed time and low five figures of spend, recurring annually.

There is no code in this repository and none is proposed here. This entry is research only.

## 7. AI role

**AI adds little to the core workflow, and I would state that plainly to a prospect.**

Hash matching is deterministic by regulatory design. CalPrivacy prescribes exact standardization so that fuzzy matching is neither required nor wanted. The In-House Privacy comment letter argues explicitly against probabilistic matching: "a data broker should be able to match any 'deterministic' data that precisely matches the data in its systems, but not 'probabilistic' data that may be associated with additional records." Source: [In-House Privacy comments](https://www.inhouseprivacy.com/blog/in-house-privacys-comments-to-the-cppa-re-sb-362-delete-act-rulemaking-on-the-subject-of-the-accessible-deletion-mechanism).

Introducing a model into the matching step would be a liability, not a feature. Any nondeterminism in a step whose output is reported to a regulator and later audited is a defect.

The two places AI earns its keep are both peripheral:

- **Schema mapping at onboarding.** Reading an unfamiliar customer CSV or database schema and proposing which columns map to which of the six DROP identifier types, with the human confirming. This is a one-time task per customer where being approximately right and reviewable is fine.
- **Exemption triage drafting.** Producing a first-pass rationale for why a given record is claimed Exempted (FCRA, GLBA, HIPAA, or the § 7613 first-party exemption), for a human to review and sign. Verified that these exemptions exist: FCRA, HIPAA, and GLBA entities are excluded from the data broker definition per [Alston & Bird](https://www.alstonprivacy.com/drop-is-coming-due-what-californias-delete-act-means-for-data-brokers-in-august/); the § 7613 first-party exemption is described by [DataGrail](https://www.datagrail.io/solutions/drop-compliance/).

This satisfies the repo rule that AI must serve a specific workflow advantage. It does, but a narrow one, and the product is viable without it.

## 8. Pricing

**Proposed model:** flat monthly subscription per registered legal entity, no per-request metering. Per-request pricing would be perverse here, because the customer's request volume is set by the state and grows without their consent.

**Proposed price points (Inference, unvalidated):**

| Tier | Price | Scope |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solo | USD 199/month | One entity, one deletion list, up to 2 identifier types, manual CSV upload of hashes |
| Standard | USD 499/month | One entity, all six lists, scheduled API cycle, suppression ledger, audit binder |
| Multi-entity | USD 999/month | Several registered legal entities under one operator |

**Anchors, all verified:**

- The customer already pays USD 6,000 per year in 2026 registration fees, rising to an approved USD 9,500 for 2027. Sources: [privacy.ca.gov](https://privacy.ca.gov/drop-for-data-brokers/), [IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/calprivacy-discusses-drop-enforcement-data-broker-fee-hike). USD 499/month is USD 5,988/year, which sits just under the existing accepted annual compliance line item. That is a defensible frame: "less than your registration fee."
- The downside being insured against is USD 200 per request per day, uncapped. Source: [privacy.ca.gov](https://privacy.ca.gov/drop-for-data-brokers/). Ketch publishes an exposure table showing 500 unprocessed requests over 30 days equals USD 3,000,000. Source: [Ketch blog](https://www.ketch.com/blog/posts/california-drop-platform-data-broker-compliance).
- The ceiling is set by Ketch Pro and DataGrail, both quote-based and enterprise-positioned, with no public figure. Ketch's highest published tier is USD 499/month and does not include DSR automation. Source: [Ketch pricing](https://www.ketch.com/pricing).

**Inference:** the honest gap in the market is a publicly priced, self-serve product between "free and manual" and "call sales." Nobody I found occupies it.

**Assumption:** that a small pass-through broker will pay USD 199 to USD 499 per month for this. **Completely unverified.** It is the single number that decides whether this is a business, and it is knowable in two weeks.

## 9. Distribution

This is the second-strongest section. The distribution problem that usually kills B2B compliance micro-SaaS is largely solved here by the regulator.

### Verified evidence

The California Data Broker Registry is published as a downloadable CSV at [cppa.ca.gov/data_broker_registry/registry.csv](https://cppa.ca.gov/data_broker_registry/registry.csv). I retrieved it. The columns include, per broker: legal name, DBA, primary website, **primary contact email address**, primary phone number, full street address, and the URL where consumers exercise privacy rights. Prior year registries for 2020 to 2025 are also downloadable.

The registry also publishes, per broker, prior-year request-handling metrics: total delete requests received, complied in whole, complied in part, denied, and mean and median days to respond. Verified by direct inspection of the CSV rows.

**Concrete first-100 channel, in priority order:**

1. **Segment the registry by revealed distress.** The 2026 registry contains each broker's own reported CCPA numbers. In the rows I inspected, one broker reported 62,584 delete requests received with 394 complied in whole and 62,190 denied, while another reported 123,378 received and 123,378 complied in whole. A broker with a high denial ratio and a small footprint is the highest-probability buyer for a matching tool. This segmentation is free and nobody else appears to be doing it.
2. **Ship the open source hashing library and rank for the exact query.** A broker's engineer or contractor searching for how to implement CalPrivacy's standardization rules is the highest-intent traffic that exists for this product.
3. **Publish a free DROP cycle deadline calculator and a public "am I in scope" checker.** The scope question is the one Ketch says most organizations get wrong.
4. **Direct outreach to the registry emails**, framed around the 45-day clock and the 2028 audit, not around features.
5. **Partner with the privacy law firms who advise this exact segment.** In-House Privacy is a named, identified firm that says it "serves many companies in the advertising and marketing industry" and has been the loudest public advocate for small brokers through two rulemaking cycles. That is a referral channel with a demonstrated point of view.
6. **File comments in the open 2028 audit rulemaking.** The comment period is live. Public comments are published with attribution. It is a credibility channel and a free reason to talk to every serious participant in this market.

### Inference

- Reasoning from the registry being a complete, contactable, government-maintained list of every buyer: customer acquisition cost here should be unusually low and unusually predictable. There is no discovery problem. There is only a persuasion problem.

### Assumptions

- That registry contact emails reach a human who can make a purchase decision rather than a `privacy@` alias routed to a ticket queue. In the rows I inspected the addresses skewed toward `privacy@` and `legal@`, which is a real deliverability and routing concern. A few rows showed named individuals.

## 10. Risks

Ordered by how likely each is to kill the business.

1. **The 70 percent do not buy.** The unserved pool may be unserved because it will never pay, not because nothing suitable exists. A broker facing a rising USD 9,500 fee may deregister, exit California, or simply absorb the enforcement risk. This is the primary risk.
2. **The free manual path is legally sufficient.** CalPrivacy provides the UI and the hashing sandbox at no cost. For a broker with one list and a few thousand records, a spreadsheet plus the DROP web interface is compliant. The product has to beat free for a customer whose alternative genuinely works.
3. **Trust and assurance gate.** The buyer is a company under active regulatory scrutiny for data handling. SOC 2 Type II and ISO 27001 are table stakes in this category (both Ketch and DataGrail advertise them prominently). Local-only hashing narrows the scope of that conversation but does not remove it. This is months of elapsed time before the first serious customer will sign.
4. **Hard market cap.** Roughly 500 to 600 registered entities. Even at strong penetration this is a lifestyle business, not a scaling one, until Connecticut arrives in 2028. That is acceptable for a solo builder but it must be entered with eyes open.
5. **Single point of dependency.** One state, one agency, one API, one specification. A CalPrivacy decision to ship free reference tooling, extend a cure period, or materially change the spec resets the product overnight. Note that In-House Privacy formally requested a one-year cure period; if granted, urgency collapses.
6. **Incumbent downmarket move.** DataGrail could add a self-serve tier at any time. They already have the module, the brand, the certifications, and the SEO.
7. **The wedge segment shrinks by design.** If CalPrivacy eventually grants the "pass-through broker" relief that In-House Privacy has requested twice, the exact segment being targeted gets a lighter obligation and needs less tooling.

## 11. Kill criteria

Falsifiable, with thresholds, ordered so the cheapest test runs first. None requires writing product code.

| # | Test | Kill threshold | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| K1 | Email 100 brokers from the registry, segmented by the distress signal in §9. Offer a paid pilot at USD 299/month, not a free beta. | Fewer than 8 replies, or fewer than 3 willing to take a paid pilot call, inside 14 days. **Kill.** | 2 weeks, no code |
| K2 | On those calls, ask directly how they processed the August cycle. | If more than half say "spreadsheet, took under two hours, fine," the manual path wins and the pain is not real at this segment. **Kill.** | Included in K1 |
| K3 | Ask what they are already paying, to whom. | If more than half already have a vendor or a retained contractor doing it, the gap is closed. **Kill.** | Included in K1 |
| K4 | Price probe at USD 499/month. | If nobody clears USD 199/month, unit economics cannot support the SOC 2 and support burden against a ~600-entity ceiling. **Kill.** | Included in K1 |
| K5 | Confirm the § 7610(a)(1) agent reading against the primary regulation and FSOR text, not a search summary. | If a third party cannot lawfully operate DROP on a broker's behalf, the hosted runner is illegal as designed. **Kill.** | 1 day, free |
| K6 | Track the 2028 audit rulemaking outcome and any cure-period grant through the 45-day comment period. | If a one-year-plus cure period is granted, urgency and therefore willingness to pay collapse. **Downgrade, revisit in 2027.** | Ongoing, free |
| K7 | Track registry size at the January 2027 registration deadline, after the fee rises to USD 9,500. | If registrant count falls more than 20 percent, the market is contracting rather than growing. **Kill.** | Free, one check |
| K8 | Watch for a self-serve, publicly priced DROP tier from DataGrail, Ketch, or Transcend. | If one launches under USD 500/month before the MVP ships. **Kill.** | Ongoing, free |

**Signals that would upgrade this to Build:** three or more paid pilot commitments from K1 at USD 299/month or above, plus a clean K5 confirmation, plus registry count holding steady into 2027.

## 12. Verdict: Watch

**Watch**, with an unusually cheap and well-defined path to Build.

**What drove the verdict toward Build.** The demand-side evidence here is the strongest I have seen in this journal for a compliance workflow. The obligation is mandatory, recurring on a fixed 45-day clock, permanent, and escalating on a published schedule through 2028. The regulator itself published the size of the gap: 30 percent adoption in week one against 450,000 pending requests. The technical specification, OpenAPI schema, and a sandbox are all free and public, so build risk is genuinely low. The complete buyer list, with contact emails and prior-year distress metrics, is a downloadable government CSV, which removes the customer-discovery problem that normally sinks solo B2B. The buyer already pays a four-figure annual fee for this exact obligation, so the budget line exists. And a specific, named practitioner has twice put the small-broker burden on the public record.

**What held it at Watch.** Every remaining doubt is on the supply side, and each one is individually survivable but they compound:

- The wedge sits between a **free path the regulator actively supports** and **funded incumbents who shipped first**. A product must beat free below and trust above, simultaneously, in a market capped at roughly 600 entities.
- The buyer is, by the nature of their business, the most assurance-sensitive customer a solo vendor could pick. SOC 2 is not optional here, and it is a months-long gate before revenue.
- The load-bearing assumption of the whole thesis, that the non-processing 70 percent represents unserved demand rather than non-buyers, is **completely unverified**. The IAPP report gives the number and not the reason.
- The legal foundation of the hosted design, that a third party may operate DROP on a broker's behalf under § 7610(a)(1), currently rests on a search summary of the FSOR rather than the primary text. That is not good enough to build on.

Calling this Build today would mean committing several months and a SOC 2 budget to a capped market on the strength of an assumption that two weeks of email would settle. That is the wrong order of operations. The correct move is K1 through K5, which cost nothing but time and produce a decisive answer.

I want to be explicit about the asymmetry in my own reading: I am more confident that this workflow is painful than that anyone will pay a stranger to fix it. Those are different claims and only the first one is evidenced.
