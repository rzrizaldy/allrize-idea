# Demand-Charge and Rate-Schedule Optimization for Small US Commercial Electricity Customers — 2026-08-26

**Contributor:** Claude (Cowork scheduled task)
**File:** `trend-journal/2026-08-26-claude.md`
**Market:** United States first. Indonesia relevance: not established. The mechanism depends on US retail tariff structures, US utility data-access regimes, and the US energy-broker channel, none of which transfer.
**Motion:** B2B. A small multi-site commercial electricity customer would pay (restaurant group, car wash chain, gym, cold storage, church, small manufacturer). The owner or facility manager would use it. Energy brokers and consultants are the alternative buyer and the obvious acquisition intermediary, neither validated.
**Verdict:** **Reject**

---

## Summary

US commercial electricity prices are rising fast and the increase is verifiably structural rather than seasonal. Demand charges, which bill on the single highest 15-minute or 30-minute power draw in a billing period rather than on total consumption, are spreading to customer classes that never faced them and are generating real, documented anger. The obvious solo-SaaS shape is a rate audit: ingest twelve months of bills or interval data, tell the customer they are on the wrong tariff and what their demand peaks actually cost, and charge a monthly fee.

I researched that shape and I am rejecting it. Not because the pain is fake, it is well evidenced, but because every layer of the product is already owned by someone who gives it away or sells it better. The tariff database layer belongs to funded incumbents claiming 97 percent US tariff coverage and 70,000 rate updates per month. The bill calculation engine is free, open source, and pip-installable. The audit service is sold by consultants at zero upfront cost on a contingency of the savings. The self-serve small-building version already exists as a named product from at least two companies. And underneath all of that sits a unit-economics problem the competitive picture only makes worse: switching a customer to the right rate schedule is a one-time job, so the recurring revenue has to come from ongoing demand coaching, which costs real money per meter to feed with data and produces advice that a small operator frequently cannot act on.

The question is whether rising commercial power cost is a lasting shift or a spike. The evidence says lasting, and it says so through 2027 at minimum.

### Verified evidence

Related queries for the same pull: top were `demand charge management` (100) and `demand charge definition` (89); the only rising query returned was `demand charge management` (50).

**EIA data reported by Utility Dive, published 2026-04-24.** "Total average revenues per kWh increased by 9.0% from last February, to 14.36 cents/kWh in February 2026." Broken out by sector year over year: transportation up 23.6 percent, commercial up 10.7 percent, industrial up 8.6 percent, residential up 7.4 percent. By state, revenue per kWh rose 26.3 percent in Virginia, 21.9 percent in Ohio, and 19.5 percent in Pennsylvania. https://www.utilitydive.com/news/electricity-retail-prices-february-eia-affordability/818425/

**EIA May 2026 Short-Term Energy Outlook, reported by Utility Dive 2026-05-15.** Commercial electricity consumption is expected to surpass residential use for the first time on record in 2027. Commercial sales grow 2.2 percent to about 1,530 billion kWh in 2026, then 5.3 percent in 2027. Residential demand stays roughly flat at about 0.5 percent growth in both years. Total US consumption is almost 4,250 billion kWh in 2026, up 1.3 percent, growing 3.1 percent in 2027. EIA: "Residential prices have been growing in all regions of the United States, and we expect this trend to continue," with East Coast annual growth as high as 7 percent for the next two years. https://www.utilitydive.com/news/data-centers-electricity-residential-rates-eia/820342/

### Inference

From the EIA sector breakout plus the STEO load forecast: the commercial sector is absorbing a larger rate increase than the residential sector while also being the sector whose load is growing, which means small commercial customers are structurally on the wrong side of this and will stay there for at least the two-year forecast horizon. This is a durable shift, not a weather spike.

From the Google Trends related-query result: the fact that `demand charge management` is both the top and the only rising related query, ahead of `demand charge definition`, is weak evidence that searchers have moved past "what is this" toward "how do I deal with this." Weak because Trends related-query values are relative within a small returned set and no volume is disclosed.

### Assumptions

That the 2021 through 2025 Google Trends rise reflects commercial buyer interest rather than, for example, students, utility staff, or solar-industry professionals. The query is ambiguous and I did not segment it.

**Trend durability: passes.** This is the strongest part of the case and it is not the reason for the verdict.

## 2. Customer and job to be done

### Inference (from the pain sources in section 3, not directly surveyed)

The buyer is the owner or facility manager of a small US commercial electricity customer whose load profile is peaky: salons, car washes, restaurants with electric kitchens, gyms, small cold storage, machine shops, churches with large HVAC. Typically one to twenty meters. Monthly electric spend roughly 500 to 5,000 dollars per site.

The job is not "understand my energy usage." It is narrower and it comes in two distinct flavors that a product tends to conflate:

1. **One-time:** "My bill jumped and I do not know why. Am I even on the right rate schedule, and would a different one be cheaper given how I actually use power?"
2. **Recurring:** "Which fifteen minutes set my peak last month, and what do I change so it does not happen again?"

Job 1 is where the money is and it is done once. Job 2 recurs but its value depends entirely on whether the operator can change behavior.

### Assumptions

That small commercial customers experience this as a purchasable problem rather than a fixed cost of doing business. Unverified. I found no survey data on small commercial willingness to pay for rate analysis.

## 3. Pain evidence

### Verified evidence

**Santee Cooper commercial demand charge, South Carolina, reported by WBTW/Nexstar, article carried 2026-03.** Carolyn Taft, owner of a hair salon near The Market Common in Myrtle Beach, on new commercial rates effective April 1 with a demand charge of roughly 12 dollars applied to the highest half hour of use in the month: "I can't put out a tip jar and ask people to give me electric bill money. I'm not quite sure what to do about it." On why she cannot shift load: "I can't blow dry you after 3. You know, I can't stay here after 7 and wash 17 loads. I really am stuck between a rock and a hard place." She notes state cosmetology board requirements force hot dryer settings, hot water, and a minimum three-hour exhaust fan run after perms. Santee Cooper's Tracy Vreeland confirms the demand charge "can just be your highest half-hour of demand throughout the entire month." https://www.yahoo.com/news/market-common-business-owner-worried-214913409.html

**Ozark Electric Cooperative demand charge backlash, southwest Missouri.** Opinion piece in the Springfield News-Leader by co-op member Mitch Sims of Clever, Missouri. OEC added a "Demand Charge" line item to April bills at 3.75 dollars per kW on the 15-minute peak in a billing period. Sims: "Members are extremely upset at the new billing scheme in that it removes all ability to manage the bottom line of the bill." OEC general manager Patrick Oehlschlager is quoted saying "Demand is here to stay." State lawmakers including Sen. Mike Moon met with members in Republic, Missouri to hear complaints. https://news.yahoo.com/news/co-op-members-must-demand-083820908.html

Note on this source: it is an opinion column by an affected member, and the customer class is residential co-op members rather than commercial. I include it because it evidences the spread of demand charges into new customer classes and the political reaction, not because it evidences commercial demand.

**Gridium marketing claim, retrieved 2026-08-26 via search:** peak demand charges "can drive 40% or more of a building's monthly electricity costs." https://gridium.com/demand-management-2/

I could not search Reddit. The `reddit.com` domain is not accessible to this session's search tool, so the buyer-complains-in-their-own-words evidence in this entry rests on two news items plus one vendor claim rather than on forum threads. ## 4. Alternatives

This section is why the verdict is Reject. Every layer is taken.

### Verified evidence

**The tariff data layer is owned.** Arcadia's Signal product page, retrieved 2026-08-26, claims 30,000 tariffs covered, 97 percent coverage of US electric tariffs, 70,000 rate updates per month, and 99 percent accuracy of modelled costs compared to actual bills. Arcadia separately ships a product literally named "Rate Monitoring & Optimization" and another named "Audit & Anomaly Detection." https://www.arcadia.com/platform/tariff-energy-rate-calculator

**The free tariff data has a staleness problem that a solo builder cannot fix.** The OpenEI Utility Rate Database is free, covers "over 3,700 U.S. utilities," and is downloadable in bulk as CSV or JSON. But the page states plainly: "We update rates annually for utilities that comprise 70% of the total US electricity load. This list includes approximately 150 different utilities across the country." Page last edited 2026-08-25. https://openei.org/wiki/Utility_Rate_Database

**The calculation engine is free and open source.** `LBNL-ETA/elecprice`, from Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, installs as `pip install electricitycostcalculator`. It reads OpenEI/URDB tariff structures, handles FIXED, ENERGY, and DEMAND rate types, generates 15-minute price signals, and computes a bill from a pandas dataframe of interval consumption with a breakdown by rate type. It also handles Peak Day Pricing events. Repository state on 2026-08-26: 2 stars, 1 fork, 28 commits, 4 open issues, 1 open pull request, no published releases, Python 100 percent. Its own stated limitations: tested only for commercial buildings, no reactive power or power-factor cost, PDP credit handling is approximate, and "Data from OpenEI might not be up to date or might even be missing for a given tariff." https://github.com/LBNL-ETA/elecprice

**The self-serve small-building product exists.** Gridium Snapmeter connects to a building's smart-meter interval data through the utility website with no hardware to install and no software for IT to configure, and it surfaces upcoming peak demand alerts, bills with high variances, and days with unexpectedly high use or demand. https://gridium.com/snapmeter-help-home/ and https://gridium.com/pro-tip-snapmeter-peak-demand-monitoring/

**A funded startup already occupies the SEO channel for this exact query.** Nectar, operated by Pairly Climate, Inc., publishes per-utility rate optimization guides with the meta description "Compare utility rate schedules, analyze time-of-use pricing, and discover cost optimization strategies for every major US utility. Save 10-30% on energy costs with the right tariff." Its feature list includes "Tariff and rate optimizations", "Utility bill audits", and "Benchmarks and audits". It explicitly targets three roles including "Utility meter and cost data API — For energy brokers and software." Its site carries head-to-head comparison pages against roughly forty named competitors, including Arcadia, EnergyCAP, Gridium, Conservice, Cass, Measurabl, Brightly, EnergyPrint, Entronix, RealPage, Envizi, Persefoni, and Gravity Climate. https://nectarclimate.com/rates

**The service version costs the customer nothing upfront.** Utility bill audit consultants work on contingency, commonly quoted at 40 to 50 percent of identified savings with no advance payment, and their scope explicitly includes evaluating rate plans and recommending switches. https://utilisave.com/about-us/utility-bill-audits-value/ and http://www.costanalysts.com/services/utility-auditing/

### Verified evidence, secondary sources

The following came from search-result summaries of third-party pages rather than pages I fetched directly, so confidence is lower.

- EnergyCAP prices per meter per year starting around 5,000 dollars annually, plus implementation, and ships a "Utility Tariff Analytics" feature that compares roughly 50 daily datapoints across a year of history to recommend a tariff switch. https://www.energycap.com/pricing/ and https://www.energycap.com/energy-monitoring-software/features/utility-tariff-analytics/
- Energy Toolbase's ETB Developer is listed at 199 dollars per user per month, or about 179 dollars per user per month billed annually. I attempted to fetch the vendor pricing page directly and it was not in this session's fetchable provenance set, so this figure is from a third-party review listing. https://apps.list.solar/tools/energy-toolbase/

### Inference

Any solo product here competes simultaneously against a free data source (URDB), a free engine (elecprice), a free-to-the-customer service (contingency consultants), a well-capitalized data monopoly (Arcadia Signal), an incumbent enterprise suite (EnergyCAP), and at least two startups already doing the exact self-serve motion (Gridium, Nectar). That is not a gap. That is a wall.

## 5. Wedge

I looked for a narrow entry point where one developer beats that field. I did not find one that survives contact with the evidence. For the record, here are the three I tested and why each fails.

**Wedge A: sell the tool to the broker or consultant who does this in Excel.** Fails on verified evidence. Nectar sells a "Utility meter and cost data API — For energy brokers and software" and Arcadia sells Signal APIs to the same audience. Both are ahead on the only asset that matters for this buyer, which is tariff coverage and freshness.

**Wedge B: go single-state and single-utility, and win on tariff accuracy where URDB is stale.** Fails on Arcadia's claimed 97 percent US tariff coverage and 70,000 rate updates per month. That claim is a vendor marketing figure and I did not independently audit it, but even discounting it heavily, a solo builder maintaining tariff sheets by hand for one utility has built a consultancy, not a SaaS, and has no path to a second state.

**Wedge C: serve the municipal utilities and rural electric cooperatives that are newly introducing demand charges, which are outside URDB's ~150 annually refreshed utilities and are lowest-priority for the incumbents.** This is the only wedge with a real supply-side gap, and it is the one I would have pursued. It fails on the demand side. The Ozark Electric case shows the affected members are residential and low-income co-op members organizing politically and voting in board elections, not businesses shopping for software. The addressable commercial subset behind a single co-op is a few hundred accounts with sub-1,000-dollar monthly bills. There is no hundred-customer channel there at a price that supports a business.

## 6. MVP

Recorded for completeness even though the verdict is Reject, because the build effort finding is itself the point.

Smallest shippable scope: upload twelve months of PDF bills or a Green Button CSV, parse consumption and demand, pull candidate tariffs for the customer's utility from the URDB bulk download, re-cost the year against each candidate using `electricitycostcalculator`, and return a ranked switch recommendation plus a peak-events report showing which intervals set each month's billed demand.

### Inference

Realistic build effort for one strong developer: roughly four to six weeks to a working single-utility demo, with PDF bill parsing across utility formats being the dominant and open-ended cost. Note the direction of that estimate. It is short precisely because the hard parts are free, which is the same fact that makes the product undefensible. Low build effort in a crowded market is a warning, not an advantage.

## 7. AI role

**AI adds little here that matters, and I want to state that plainly rather than manufacture a role for it.**

The genuinely useful AI application is parsing heterogeneous utility bill PDFs into structured line items, which is a real and annoying problem that a vision-capable model handles better than templated extraction. That is a cost reducer, not a moat, and it is available to every competitor at the same price.

The analysis itself is deterministic arithmetic over a tariff structure and an interval series. Running it through a language model would make it slower, more expensive, and less correct. The one thing a small operator actually needs, which is a plan for shifting load that respects the constraints of their business, is exactly what the salon-owner quote in section 3 shows an AI cannot supply: her constraints are a state cosmetology board and customer arrival times, not a scheduling problem.

## 8. Pricing

Recorded for completeness. Anchors, all from section 4.

- Free: URDB data, `electricitycostcalculator`, Nectar's public rate guides.
- Free to the customer upfront: contingency bill audit at 40 to 50 percent of savings.
- Data cost floor: UtilityAPI charges 12 dollars flat for 8 to 12 months of on-demand bill or interval collection per meter, and 3.00 dollars per meter per month for monthly Ongoing Monitoring on pay-as-you-go, falling to 2.40 dollars at the 3,000-dollar prepaid tier. Weekly monitoring is 12.00 dollars per meter per month and daily is 30.00 dollars, before volume discounts. Data is free where UtilityAPI has a utility partnership. Pricing page last modified 2026-04-09. https://utilityapi.com/pricing
- Enterprise: EnergyCAP from roughly 5,000 dollars per year plus implementation. Energy Toolbase ETB Developer at roughly 199 dollars per user per month.

### Inference

A plausible solo price point is 39 to 79 dollars per site per month, or a 149 to 299 dollar one-time audit. Now put the data floor next to it. Weekly interval monitoring alone costs 12.00 dollars per meter per month at list, which is 15 to 31 percent of a 39-to-79-dollar subscription before any other cost, and the customer whose recurring value proposition justifies that monitoring is the one who cannot act on the result. The one-time audit prices better against the pain but produces no second sale.

**This is the second independent reason for the verdict.** Even with no competition, the recurring version of this product has a hostile cost structure and the profitable version has no retention.

## 10. Risks

1. **Tariff data staleness produces a wrong recommendation.** URDB refreshes only about 150 utilities annually. A recommendation to switch schedules based on a superseded tariff creates a customer who pays more after following your advice. For a solo product with no errors-and-omissions insurance and no support staff, one such case is reputationally fatal.
2. **Bill and interval data access is fragile and metered.** Green Button Connect My Data is not universally deployed. Per a search-result summary of UtilityAPI's Green Button documentation, "only the CA IOUs and ComEd have a Connect My Data process in place." I did not fetch that documentation page directly, so treat this as lower confidence, but if it is even approximately right, national coverage means depending on a vendor that scrapes utility portals, which breaks when portals change and costs per meter per month regardless.
3. **The value is one-time.** Once the customer switches rate schedules the recurring job shrinks to peak monitoring, which is lower value and higher data cost.
4. **The customer often cannot act.** Documented directly in the salon-owner quote. A tool that reliably tells a business something true and unactionable churns.
5. **Incumbent bundling.** Arcadia and EnergyCAP already ship rate optimization as a feature. Nectar ships it alongside bill audits and payments.
6. **Regulatory whiplash.** Demand charges are drawing political attention, as in the Missouri legislator meetings. A tariff redesign in response would invalidate a chunk of the analysis.

## 11. Kill criteria

Falsifiable thresholds, written as they would have been applied before building.

- **Killed if** a funded competitor already ships the exact wedge under a product name. **Result: triggered.** Arcadia ships "Rate Monitoring & Optimization." Nectar ships "Tariff and rate optimizations." Gridium ships peak demand alerts from utility-portal interval data.
- **Killed if** the core calculation is available as free open source that a competitor could adopt in under a week. **Result: triggered.** `pip install electricitycostcalculator`.
- **Killed if** the incumbent service alternative reaches the customer at zero upfront cost. **Result: triggered.** Contingency bill audits at 40 to 50 percent of savings.
- **Killed if** recurring data cost per customer exceeds 20 percent of the plausible subscription price. **Result: triggered at weekly monitoring frequency** (12.00 dollars per meter per month against a 39-to-79-dollar subscription). Not triggered at monthly frequency (3.00 dollars), but monthly frequency is too coarse to prevent a peak that has already happened.
- **Not triggered:** trend durability. Would have required flat or declining commercial price growth. The opposite is true.
- **Not assessed:** willingness to pay. Would have required 10 of 20 cold-called small commercial operators to name a dollar figure. Not run, and the verdict does not depend on it, because four other criteria already fired.

## 12. Verdict: Reject

Rejecting a real problem is uncomfortable and worth being explicit about. The trend is the best-evidenced trend in this journal to date: commercial revenue per kWh up 10.7 percent year over year in February 2026, commercial load overtaking residential in 2027, and five straight years of rising search interest in demand charges. The pain is documented in a named business owner's own words.

The reason to reject is that none of that accrues to a solo builder. The problem's value is captured by whoever owns the tariff data, and that is Arcadia at a claimed 97 percent coverage and 70,000 monthly rate updates, or the contingency consultant who takes half the savings and asks for nothing upfront. What is left for a new entrant is a free engine, a stale free database, a one-time job, and a customer who often cannot act on the answer.

Two things would reopen this, and both are supply-side, not demand-side:

1. **A machine-readable tariff publication mandate.** The URDB page states that NREL/NLR "has formed working groups with utilities and industry to explore options for adopting a standard machine-readable format that utilities could use to publish their rates." If that becomes a requirement rather than a working group, the tariff data moat collapses and the incumbents' 97-percent-coverage advantage goes to zero overnight. That is the single event to watch.
2. **Green Button Connect My Data reaching broad national deployment.** If authorized bill and interval access becomes free and universal instead of vendor-metered, the per-meter cost floor in section 8 disappears and the recurring product becomes viable at 39 dollars per month.

Until one of those happens, this is a good problem in a bad position. Watching the first one is cheap and I would recommend the repo check the URDB machine-readable-format working group once a quarter.
